## Beat markers

A `||` inside a paragraph marks where the next beat appears on the
slide. It is editorial, never spoken: every tool strips it before the
text reaches the generator or the aligner.

They exist because the script is written one paragraph per slide, so
nothing otherwise records which words belong to which beat. With a
marker, a cue is arithmetic: count words to the marker, read that
word's timestamp. Without one it has to be inferred, and inference put
cues ten to twenty-five seconds past the moment the voice had already
moved on.

A slide with n beats carries n-1 markers. `scripts/derive-cues.py`
uses them when they are there, falls back to inference when they are
not, and says which it did.

Modules 3 to 6 had theirs backfilled from cues already measured by
hand. Modules 7 and 8 were marked before any cue existed, by reading
each slide's beats beside the narration's sentences, so all 55 of
their beats derived as `marked` and none as a guess.

Markers cannot express a delayed first beat. The last slide of
Modules 3 and 4 holds its content back until 16.9s and 24.9s, letting
the voice speak first, and that is a time rather than a word
boundary. Both stay hand-set, and `scripts/apply-cues.py` takes them
as overrides.

## Course Intro

**1.** Welcome. This is Build Your Marketing Foundation. If you’re here, you have a service business with paying clients. You’ve been at it long enough to know that marketing advice comes at you constantly, and most of it doesn’t fit. That’s what this course is for.

**2.** In about three hours, you’ll build a working marketing foundation. Three layers. Seven foundations. Eight modules. One plan that’s actually yours. Let’s get into it.

**3.** Here’s the shape of what you’ll be building. Three layers. Each one does a different job.

**4.** Layer one is Get Found. The infrastructure that makes you discoverable to the people who need what you do. Layer two is Get Chosen. The signals that make someone pick you over the alternatives. Layer three is Be Remembered. The work that keeps you present with people who aren’t ready to buy yet.

**5.** There are seven foundations total. The first is positioning, and it’s where the course starts, not because it outranks the rest, but because it’s the upstream answer the rest depend on. The other six sit across the three layers: getting found, earned proof, referrals, awareness, owned audience, and authority. Every service business needs all seven. Not every service business needs to build them in the same order. That’s what the audit is for.

**6.** You’ll be working with a tool called the Marketing Foundation Map. Think of it as your working document, built into the course itself. When it’s time to do the work, the tool is right there on the slide. Your positioning statement, your diagnostic and audit results, and the strategy you set in each foundation module all save automatically and live in one place. There’s nothing to open and nothing to manage. Do the work where the course asks for it, and the Map keeps it.

**7.** Here’s what you’ll do across the eight modules.

**8.** Module 1 is Positioning. Three questions, one working statement. Module 2 is the Foundation Audit. Six questions, then the audit itself. You’ll leave with a priority order, a gap list, and a clear starting point for your build. Modules 3 through 8 cover the remaining six foundations. Each module goes deep on one specific foundation: getting found, earned proof, referrals, awareness, owned audience, and authority. You’ll take all six, even if the audit shows that some need your attention sooner than others. Module 8 does double work. It teaches the final foundation, authority building, and then closes the course. You’ll return to everything you’ve set in the Marketing Foundation Map and sequence all seven foundations into one plan. First. Next. Later. Sized to the hours you actually have.

**9.** But positioning comes first. Everything that follows in this course depends on having a clear positioning statement. The diagnostic uses it. The audit references it. The remaining foundation modules are built around it. Don’t skip ahead. Don’t skim Module 1. Take the time, do the work, and meet me there.

## Module 1: Positioning

**1.** Every other module in this course is going to ask you to make decisions that depend on three answers you may not have yet. Decisions about your website. Your reviews. Your local listings. Your visibility. Your email list. Each of those modules will ask you to make calls about who you’re talking to, what you’re offering, and what makes you the right choice. If you don’t have working answers to those questions, every decision downstream gets harder. That’s why we start here. It probably isn’t the most important thing, depending on your business. It is the upstream answer everything else depends on. First in order, not first in importance.

**2.** The three questions: Who do you serve. What do you do. What makes you different. That’s the module.

**3.** There are longer positioning frameworks. Some of them are excellent. If you’ve taken a brand strategy course, you’ve probably encountered ten-question worksheets, audience archetype builders, voice-and-tone matrices. Those have their place. They’re not what you need first. What you need first is enough clarity to make the rest of the foundation work coherent. That’s what these three questions produce. Not a polished brand. Not a final positioning statement you’d put on a billboard. A working answer to who you are, what you do, and what makes you you. Specific enough to write a website tagline, fill out a Google Business Profile, ask for the right kind of reviews. Foundation-level positioning. Not deep brand strategy. If during this module you realize you want to go further, into a fully developed brand voice, a richer positioning system, a deeper differentiation framework, that’s a real instinct, and worth following. But not in this module. This module gets you to good enough to keep moving.

**4.** A few things I see, consistently, when service business owners answer these questions. The first is vagueness in the name of inclusivity. “I serve small businesses.” “I help busy professionals.” “I work with anyone who needs my service.” These feel safe because they don’t exclude anyone. They also don’t do any work. A vague answer to who you serve produces a vague website, vague reviews, vague referrals. The cost of being too narrow is a smaller addressable market. The cost of being too vague is being chosen by no one. The second is generic differentiation. “I care about quality.” “I take a personal approach.” “I have years of experience.” These claims are real, but they’re also things every competent business says. They don’t differentiate you. If your competitor could put your differentiator on their own website word-for-word, it’s not a differentiator. The third is describing yourself instead of your service. “I’m a marketing consultant.” “I’m a chiropractor.” That’s a category, not a service. The service is what you actually do for a person. The problem you solve. The outcome you produce. Three patterns to watch for. We’ll come back to each one when we get to its question.

**5.** Here’s how the next 25 minutes of teaching go, and there’s writing time on top of that. Five minutes on each of the three questions. Then three minutes at the end to assemble the answers into one sentence. By the end of the module, you’ll have a working positioning statement. For each question, I’ll give you the framework, then show you what a weak answer looks like, then show you what a strong answer looks like across five different service businesses, a therapist, a pressure washer, a photographer, a chiropractor, and a vivids stylist. The framework is the same. The answers are different. Then we synthesize.

**6.** Let’s start with the question that everything else depends on. Who do you serve?

**7.** Who do you serve is not a demographic question. It’s an operational one. The question isn’t “what age range and gender are my customers.” That answer rarely changes a marketing decision. The question is closer to: when I imagine the person I most want walking through my door, what’s specifically true about them? What kind of work are they doing? What kind of life are they in? What problem brought them looking? The test of a good answer is simple: would two competitors answer this question identically? If your answer applies to every business in your category, it’s not specific enough.

**8.** Three patterns I see most often. The first is demographic-only. “Women 35 to 55.” “Small business owners.” “Local homeowners.” These describe a population, not a person. Every business in your category serves this same population. The answer doesn’t help you decide anything. The second is needs-based without specificity. “People who want quality work.” “People who value good service.” “People who care about their results.” These are universal. No customer says they want bad work or doesn’t care about results. Naming the obvious isn’t naming your audience. The third is every-customer-you’ve-ever-had. The instinct to include everyone you’ve worked with, because each one paid you. That’s an inclusive description, but it’s also a description of your past, not your strategy. A good answer to who you serve narrows your strategic audience without rejecting customers who happen to fall outside it.

**9.** A useful answer to “who do you serve” has three layers. First, the situation they’re in. Where they are in their life or business. A solo professional, a growing team, a homeowner with a property they’ve owned five years, a couple planning their wedding. The situational frame. Second, the work or life context. What they’re doing day-to-day that brings them to need your service. Not the demographic, but the active context. A therapist’s audience isn’t “anxious people”, it’s “high-functioning professionals managing chronic anxiety while running demanding careers.” The active context is what makes the description usable. Third, the problem they came in with. What pulled them to look for someone like you. Not “they need pressure washing.” Closer to: “their property is showing wear after years they didn’t budget time to maintain it, and they don’t want to spend a Saturday doing it themselves.” Situation. Context. Problem. Three layers, each adding specificity.

**10.** Here’s how the five personas in this course answer it. Maya, the therapist. “I serve high-functioning professionals, typically 30s to 50s, in demanding careers, who are managing chronic anxiety and want a therapist who treats them as competent adults rather than fragile.” The situation is established, the context is named, the problem under the problem is surfaced. Marcus, the pressure washer. “I serve homeowners who’ve owned their property at least three years and are starting to notice the buildup, but don’t want to spend a Saturday on a ladder.” Situation: long-term homeowners. Context: they’ve started noticing wear. Problem: time, not money. Lena, the photographer. “I serve couples planning weddings in the four-to-eight-thousand-dollar photography range, who want a documentary style and have already decided their wedding doesn’t need to be picture-perfect to be worth photographing.” Specific budget tier. Style preference. A values position, they self-select. James, the chiropractor. “I serve adults who’ve had a recent injury or pain event and want to address it without a long-term care plan they didn’t ask for.” The problem under the problem: they don’t want to be sold ongoing care. Sasha, the vivids stylist. “I serve women in their 20s and 30s who’ve been growing out their hair for a vivid transformation, who care about color theory and hair health, and who’ll travel an hour or more to find a stylist they trust with fashion color.” Visual proof matters. Audience is specific. They self-select on willingness to travel.

**11.** Naming a strategic audience doesn’t mean turning away customers who walk through your door. It means deciding who your marketing is for. Specificity in marketing doesn’t restrict your business. It only restricts your marketing.

**12.** Your turn. Pause here, this one needs a couple of real minutes, not thirty seconds. Write a rough first pass at “who do you serve” using the three-layer framework: situation, context, problem. You are not perfecting it. You are getting a working answer down that the rest of the module can sharpen. When you have something, come back.

**13.** What do you do?

**14.** Most service business owners answer this question with their category. I’m a chiropractor. I’m a wedding photographer. I’m a marketing consultant. That’s not an answer to what you do. That’s an answer to what you are. The category locates you in a market. It doesn’t describe the work. What you actually do is the work you perform on a person’s behalf. The chiropractor doesn’t sell chiropractic, she relieves a specific kind of pain in a specific kind of patient. The wedding photographer doesn’t sell photos, she captures a wedding day in a particular style for a particular kind of couple. The category is what you offer. The service is what happens when someone hires you.

**15.** Three weak patterns to watch for. The first is category-only. “I’m a chiropractor.” “I do branding.” “I clean houses.” These are accurate but they don’t do any work. They tell the buyer what aisle of the marketplace you’re in. They don’t tell the buyer what they’re getting. The second is generic help statements. “I help businesses grow.” “I help people feel better.” Help-statements feel substantive but they’re often empty. Every business in your category helps with the same general thing. The third is full-service language. “I do everything from X to Y to Z.” Listing your services is a price-sheet move, not a positioning move.

**16.** A useful answer to what you do has three layers. First, the action. What you actually do during the engagement. Not the category. The verbs. A chiropractor adjusts. A photographer captures and edits. A consultant audits and rebuilds. Second, the output. What the client walks away with. A treatment plan and a measurable reduction in pain. A complete wedding gallery delivered in six weeks. A documented strategy and a 90-day plan. Third, the change. What’s true about the client’s life or business after the work that wasn’t true before. The chiropractor’s patient can carry their child without flinching. Action. Output. Change.

**17.** Here’s how the five personas answer it. Maya. “I work with clients in weekly fifty-minute sessions over three to twelve months, using cognitive behavioral methods adapted for high-performing professionals. They leave with a working set of tools for managing the patterns that used to derail them.” Marcus. “I clean exterior surfaces, siding, driveways, decks, fences, using commercial-grade equipment in a single visit, usually four to six hours on-site. The client walks out the next morning to a property that looks five years younger.” Lena. “I document wedding days from the morning preparation through the reception, in a candid documentary style with minimal posing. Clients receive a complete edited gallery within six weeks, typically four to six hundred images.” James. “I assess the source of the injury or pain in the first visit, then design a treatment plan with a defined endpoint, usually six to twelve sessions. Patients leave with a measurable reduction in pain, an understanding of what caused the issue, and the strength to keep it from coming back.” Sasha. “I do color transformations using fashion colors, vivid reds, blues, pinks, neons, across single 6-to-10-hour sessions, with hair-health protocols that protect integrity through repeated processing. Clients walk out with the color they followed me on Instagram for, and the structural strength to maintain it.”

**18.** Describing your service specifically doesn’t mean refusing services you also offer. The specific description is what your marketing leads with, not the boundary of what you’ll do. A vague service description doesn’t expand your business. It just makes your marketing harder to remember.

**19.** Your turn. Pause here and give this a couple of real minutes. Use the three-layer framework: action, output, change. You are not perfecting it, you are getting a working answer down. Come back when you have one.

**20.** What makes you different?

**21.** The third question is the one where most positioning attempts fall apart. The reason is a quiet confusion. Most service business owners hear what makes you different and answer the question of what makes you better. Those are not the same question. Better is comparative and unverifiable. I care more. I’m more thorough. I have higher standards. If your competitor could write your differentiator on their own website without changing a word, it’s not a differentiator. Different is descriptive and falsifiable. It names a specific approach, philosophy, constraint, or method that another business in your category genuinely does not operate with. Different doesn’t have to mean better. The buyer’s job isn’t to decide which business is best. It’s to decide which business is for them. Different is what helps them decide.

**22.** Three weak patterns. The first is quality claims. “I care about quality.” “I take a personal approach.” “I have high standards.” Baseline expectations, not differentiators. The second is credentials as differentiation. “I have fifteen years of experience.” “I’ve worked with hundreds of clients.” Credentials answer should I trust you, not why should I choose you over someone else. The third is false uniqueness. “I’m the only one who really understands my clients.” “Nobody does it like I do.” Confidence with no evidence.

**23.** A real differentiator usually comes from one of four places. Approach. The actual method or philosophy you use that another business in your category doesn’t. Constraint. What you deliberately don’t do. Constraints are powerful differentiators because most competitors are afraid to constrain. Combination. The specific combination of skills, perspectives, or background that you bring that someone else couldn’t easily replicate. Position. A clear stance against something common in your category.

**24.** Maya. “I work with clients on a defined timeline with the explicit goal of ending therapy, not extending it. I treat my clients as competent adults who can handle directness, not fragile patients.” A constraint plus an approach. Both push against category defaults. Marcus. “I show up when I say I will, complete the job in the window I quoted, and don’t upsell during the visit. The price you got is the price you pay.” A position against the most common complaints customers have about contractors. Lena. “I shoot in a documentary style and I don’t do extensive posing. If you want a wedding with thirty staged photographs of the bridal party in coordinated poses, I’m not the right photographer for you.” Constraint plus position. The “I’m not the right photographer” line is the strongest move. James. “I work with a defined treatment plan and a stated endpoint. I don’t sell ongoing care plans, monthly memberships, or maintenance subscriptions. When the work is done, you stop coming.” A constraint against the dominant business model in chiropractic care. Sasha. “Vivids only. No balayage, no foils, no special-occasion blowouts. Hair-health-first protocol, including the willingness to refuse a transformation if I think the hair can’t sustain it without damage.” Constraint plus position. The “I’ll refuse if it’ll damage your hair” is the strongest move.

**25.** What if you really are better than your competitors? Some of you probably are. The answer isn’t to claim it. The answer is to be specific about what kind of better. Better at what, exactly. For whom, exactly. Most of the time, what you find is closer to different than to more of the same.

**26.** The strongest differentiators in service businesses usually come from rejecting something common in the category, not from claiming to be better at it. Maya rejected open-ended therapy. Marcus rejected upselling. Lena rejected staged posing. James rejected subscription care. Sasha rejected anything but vivids. Notice the pattern. Each one is a thing competitors quietly do, and most of them feel slightly bad about. The strong differentiator is the one that names what’s wrong with the category default and offers a clean alternative. If you’re stuck on this question, ask yourself: what’s the thing competitors in my category do that I refuse to do?

**27.** A sharp differentiator will turn off some prospects. That’s the point. If your differentiator doesn’t repel anyone, it’s probably not specific enough to attract anyone either. A vague positioning attracts vague interest. A sharp positioning attracts qualified interest.

**28.** Your turn. Pause here, and give this one a few real minutes, it is the hardest of the three. Use one or more of the four sources: approach, constraint, combination, position. If you’re stuck, ask the contrarian question: what does my category do that I refuse to do? A rough first pass is the goal, not a finished line.

**29.** Last section. Let’s pull your three answers into one statement that does real work.

**30.** Your three answers combine into one sentence. The structure looks like this. “I serve [who you serve] who [the situation, context, or problem]. I [what you do, the action and output and change]. What makes me different is [your approach, constraint, combination, or position].” Three clauses. One sentence. The shape isn’t sacred. The substance is what matters.

**31.** Maya. “I work with high-functioning professionals managing chronic anxiety while running demanding careers. I deliver therapy on a defined timeline using cognitive behavioral methods, with the explicit goal of giving clients tools they take with them, not extending the relationship indefinitely. What makes me different is that I treat my clients as competent adults rather than fragile patients.” Marcus. “I serve homeowners who’ve owned their property at least three years and are starting to notice the buildup but don’t want to spend a Saturday on a ladder. I clean exterior surfaces with commercial-grade equipment in a single visit, and the price you got is the price you pay. What makes me different is that I show up when I say I will, complete the job in the window I quoted, and don’t upsell during the visit.”

**32.** Lena. “I serve couples planning weddings in the four-to-eight-thousand-dollar photography range who want a documentary style. I document wedding days in a candid style with minimal posing and deliver a complete edited gallery within six weeks. What makes me different is that I shoot documentary, not staged. If you want thirty posed photographs of the bridal party, I’m not the right photographer for you.” James. “I work with adults who’ve had a recent injury or pain event and want to address it without being sold a long-term care plan they didn’t ask for. I assess the source in the first visit and design a treatment plan with a defined endpoint, usually six to twelve sessions. What makes me different is that I don’t sell ongoing care plans or maintenance subscriptions. When the work is done, you stop coming.”

**33.** Sasha. “I serve women in their 20s and 30s who’ve been growing out their hair for a vivid transformation, who care about color theory and hair health. I do vivid color only, fashion colors across single 6-to-10-hour sessions, with hair-health protocols built in. What makes me different is that I’ll refuse a transformation if I don’t think the hair can sustain it. The color you see on my Instagram is the color you get, and the hair will still be hair when I’m done.”

**34.** Your statement is good enough when it does three things. It’s specific enough that two competitors couldn’t write the same sentence. It’s clear enough that a friend who’s never heard you describe your business could read it and know what you do. And it gives you something to write your website tagline against, fill out your Google Business Profile from, ask for the right kind of reviews around. Foundation-level positioning. Not brand strategy. The work this module does is enough to keep moving.

**35.** One step before you move on. Your working statement is already saved. You assembled it right here on the slide, and from now on it lives in the Positioning tab of your Marketing Foundation Map. There’s nothing to open and nothing to paste. The Map holds it for you, so you don’t have to keep finding it. Take a minute to read it back.

**36.** You have your working positioning statement. From here, the rest of the course can ask you to make decisions that depend on it. The next module is the audit: an honest look at where your marketing actually stands, across seven foundations and three layers. Take a breath. Then move to Module 2.

## Module 2: The Foundation Audit

**1.** Welcome to Module 2. You just finished Module 1 with a working positioning statement. The natural next question is: what do I do now? What is the first marketing move? I am going to ask you to wait on that question for the next thirty minutes. Because the answer depends on something we have not established yet: where you actually stand. And without knowing that, every tactic you pick is a guess. That is what this module produces. Not tactics. A clear-eyed picture of your starting point.

**2.** Here is the disconnect that drives most of the marketing frustration I see. The advice you find online, the courses, the YouTube videos, the LinkedIn posts, almost all of it is generic. Build an email list. Post consistently on Instagram. Get more reviews. Start a podcast. Run ads. All of it might be true for someone. Almost none of it tells you whether it applies to you. So you end up doing one of two things. You try a little of everything, spreading yourself across tactics that do not compound. Or you pick whatever feels most familiar and ignore the rest, hoping you got lucky. Neither produces results. This module does something different. Before we ask how strong is your marketing, we ask what kind of business are you running, and what does strong marketing look like for that business specifically. A therapist’s strong marketing does not look like a pressure washer’s. The foundations they need to build are different. The order they should build them in is different. Module 2 figures that out first. Then assesses you against the version of strong marketing that actually applies to your business.

**3.** Here is the shape of the work. You will answer six diagnostic questions about your business. Not preference questions, questions about what is structurally true about how you operate, who you serve, and how clients find you. Those answers will produce a priority order. Some foundations will land as high priority for your business. Some as medium. Some as low. A few may not apply at all. Then you will run an audit weighted by those priorities. High-priority foundations get rated in depth. Low-priority foundations get rated lightly. The audit does not waste your attention on things that do not matter for your business. The output is a Gap List, what is solid, what is slipping, what is missing, sorted by priority. And from that, a single starting point: the one foundation to focus on first, with the reasoning behind why.

**4.** The first piece of work in this module is a diagnostic. Six questions about what is structurally true about your business. These are not preference questions. They are not asking what you would like to be true or what you are working toward. They are asking what is true today, about your business, your audience, and how clients actually find you and decide to hire you. Your answers to these six questions are what will make the rest of the audit specific to your business. Without them, you would just be running a generic assessment. With them, the audit knows what kind of business it is looking at.

**5.** Here is a distinction worth making before you start. Most marketing advice asks what you want, what platforms you like, what tactics feel good, what kind of content you would enjoy making. Those are real questions, but they produce strategies built around your preferences. This diagnostic does not ask any of that. It asks what your business structurally requires. A therapist serving older clients in a regulated profession requires different marketing than a wedding photographer serving twenty-somethings. Not because they want different things. Because their businesses work differently. The diagnostic is mapping that. The result is harder to argue with. It is not “here is what you should do.” It is “here is what is true about your business, and here is what that implies.”

**6.** Here are the six questions, all at once. Where do your current clients first hear about you. Does your work depend on visual proof to land a sale. Are there professional or legal restrictions on how you can market. How old is your typical client. Is your service local-only, hybrid, or remote. And how long does a typical prospect engage with your work before they first reach out. That is it. Six questions. Each one is doing specific work, which I will explain in a moment. Together, they produce the priority order the audit will use. A note on time. These take about three to four minutes to answer if you are being thoughtful. If you are answering quickly, less. Do not rush them. The recommendations are only as accurate as your answers.

**7.** The first three questions are about your business itself. Question one asks where current clients first hear about you. This anchors the diagnostic in what is already working, what channel is actually producing results today. Not what you wish was working. What is. Question two asks whether your work depends on visual proof. Some businesses sell visible work, a hairstylist, a contractor, a photographer. Their portfolio matters more than their writing. Other businesses sell expertise and judgment, a therapist, a consultant, a financial advisor. Their published thinking matters more than their portfolio. The diagnostic uses this to weight whether you build through visual presence or through credibility signals. Question three asks about ethics restrictions. Therapists, attorneys, healthcare providers, financial advisors all face professional rules that restrict testimonials, paid referrals, or specific marketing claims. If that is you, the diagnostic will weight the audit accordingly. If it is not, those constraints will not apply.

**8.** The last three questions are about your audience and how they make the buying decision. Question four asks how old your typical client is. This shapes which channels actually reach them. A 55+ audience lives less on social platforms and more in email and search. A 25-year-old audience lives in the opposite places. The diagnostic uses age to weight where you should be present. Question five asks whether your service is local-only, hybrid, or remote. If clients have to come to you, local presence is foundational. If you are fully remote and serving a national audience, local presence does not apply at all, and the diagnostic will literally remove it from your audit. Question six asks how long a typical prospect engages with your work before they first reach out. Engagement is anything before contact: searching, reading, following you on social, hearing about you from others. Some prospects discover you when they need you and contact you immediately. Some follow your work for months. Long pre-contact engagement signals that sustained credibility and presence matter more than quick-trust signals. The diagnostic weights accordingly.

**9.** Two things on how to answer well. First: answer based on what is actually true, not what you wish was true. If most of your clients found you through referrals, the answer is referrals, even if you would rather they were finding you through a podcast you have not started yet. The diagnostic is mapping current reality so it can tell you where to leverage what is working and where the real gaps are. Second: if you genuinely do not know an answer, pick the option that says so. The first question has a “honestly, I don’t know” option for exactly this reason. Do not guess. A wrong answer produces wrong recommendations. An honest “I don’t know” tells the diagnostic to default to safe assumptions and prompts you to track that information going forward. When you are ready, head to the tool and answer the six questions. I will meet you back here once you have seen your priority order.

**10.** Pause here. The tool is on this slide. Answer all six questions thoughtfully, three to four minutes of work. When you reach the end and see your priority order, high, medium, low, and possibly some not-applicable, come back to the video. Section 3 will help you read what the priority order is telling you about your business.

**11.** You should be looking at your priority order now. Six foundations sorted into high, medium, low, and possibly a few marked not applicable. Each one has a short reasoning line under it explaining why it landed where it landed. Before you run the audit, take a few minutes to actually read what the diagnostic produced. The priority order is doing real strategic work, and if you skim past it, the audit that follows will not make sense.

**12.** Four priority tiers, each with a different operational meaning. High priority means this is where strategic work pays off for your business. These foundations are doing the heaviest lifting for how clients find you, evaluate you, and remember you. The audit will rate these in depth and the recommendations will focus here. **18.** Here is how the audit works, and why it is different from most self-assessments you have seen. Most marketing audits ask you to rate yourself on a scale. Each one describes a specific state, what is actually true about your website, your reviews, your referral system, your published expertise. You read the scenarios and pick the one that matches your reality. You are not rating. You are recognizing. That looks like my business. That is the question. When you rate yourself on an abstract scale, you have to translate your messy reality into a number. People are generous with themselves on abstract ratings. They are not as generous when they are picking between concrete scenarios that name specifics. Scenarios force more honest answers. That is the design.

**19.** The audit does not ask the same depth of questions across every foundation. It varies based on the priority order you saw in Section 3. **20.** Now the most important point in this entire module. This audit is going to ask you to assess your own marketing. That sounds obvious. ## Module 3: Get Found

**1.** Welcome to Module 3. Get found. This is the first of the foundation modules. Each one is a concept module, not a tactics module. The goal isn’t to send you off to build a website by Friday. The goal is to give you a working answer to a strategic question: when a prospect goes looking for what you do, which of the places they might find you actually matter for your business? Twenty-five minutes. By the end you’ll know which touchpoints are primary for your business, and you’ll have marked them in your Marketing Foundation Map. Then we move to Module 4.

**2.** The first thing to fix. Most people, when you think about getting found online, think website. So that’s the first thing to set aside. Getting found is not a website. A website is one piece of it. The whole thing is bigger, and the parts do different work.

**3.** Here’s the working definition for this module. Getting found is the sum of all the places you show up when a prospect goes looking for what you do. A friend sends them an Instagram post: that’s one of those places. They Google your category and your listing shows up: another. A directory entry. The map result when they search nearby. Your website. Every one of them is somewhere you can be found, or fail to be found. Every business has some of these. Few have all of them. None need all of them. The || strategic question is which ones yours actually needs.

**4.** There are five touchpoints in the online presence map. Each one does a specific job. Each one answers a different question for the prospect. Knowing which question goes with which touchpoint is how you decide what each one needs to do. And, just as important, what each one doesn’t need to do.

**5.** Here they are. Five touchpoints. One: your website. The place that holds everything about you. || Two: search and map listings. The place a prospect finds you when they don’t yet know your name. Google Business Profile and Apple Maps. || Three: social platforms. The place a prospect sees you before they’re actively looking for someone like you. || Four: third-party listings. The places someone else vouches for you. This is two kinds of platforms. Review platforms like Yelp, where your business shows up whether you signed up or not, because customers wrote about you. And opt-in directories like OpenTable, Psychology Today, The Knot, where you enrolled and built a profile. Different work for each, but both are third parties that vouch for your existence. || Five: online communities. Where your prospects participate with each other. Facebook groups, Reddit threads, Discord servers, niche forums. You show up as a peer, not as a brand. Each one has a different job. Let’s go through them.

**6.** Before we go deep on each touchpoint, a quick map. You’ve probably heard a lot of words. SEO. AEO. Google Business Profile. Yelp. Instagram. Ads. Local SEO. Each of those things has a place in this framework. None of them is a separate touchpoint. They all live inside the five you just saw. SEO and AEO are about how well your website performs in search. Google Business Profile and Apple Maps are your search and map listing. The social platforms are the social touchpoint. Yelp and the industry directories are the third-party listings. Ads sit across everything. They amplify any touchpoint you already have. They don’t create a new one. Hold this map in mind as we go through the five touchpoints in detail.

**7.** Touchpoint one: your website. The website is the full picture. The place that holds everything: positioning, proof, pricing, process, and the way to take the next step. When depth is needed, that’s where depth lives. Worth naming: your email list lives downstream of the website. Visitors become subscribers, subscribers get nurtured, eventually some become clients. That work belongs to the website touchpoint, not a separate one. When the website matters, your email list matters. When the website is light, the email list usually is too. When does the website || matter most? When prospects engage with you over weeks or months before they reach out. When the work is visual and needs to be seen. When the price warrants real research. When does || it matter less? When most prospects find you through Google and call within hours. When trust is established somewhere other than your site. When the work is quick and local and price-comparable. For some businesses, a website is genuinely optional. For others, it exists as a booking page and nothing more. Both are fine. Same touchpoint. Different weight, depending on the business.

**8.** Touchpoint two: search and map. This is the findable you. The version of you that exists in Google Business Profile and Apple Maps. It answers the question a prospect has before they know your name. Who, in my area, does this thing? Hours. Location. Photos. Reviews. The basic facts. That’s what this touchpoint carries. || Matters most when you’re local-only or hybrid. When the search happens before the name does. When your category is dense and reviews are how prospects choose. || Matters less when you’re remote, or when most clients come from referral, or when your category demand doesn’t translate to search behavior.

**9.** Touchpoint three: social platforms. The ambient you. The version of you that shows up in someone’s feed before they were looking. Instagram, LinkedIn, TikTok, wherever your audience already is. This one answers a question the prospect didn’t even know they had. Do I want to follow this person? It carries voice, taste, work-in-progress, the texture of how you think. Not the case for hiring you. The case for paying attention to you. || Matters most when the work is visual. When discovery happens through shared interest rather than search. When your audience overlaps with the buyer’s audience. Matters less || when prospects come from search, or referral, or industries with ethics rules that limit social. When the work isn’t visual. When the platform’s audience just doesn’t match.

**10.** Touchpoint four: third-party listings. The vouched-for you. The version of you that shows up inside someone else’s platform. A marketplace, a professional directory, a review site. Answers the question a prospect asks when they’re already inside that platform’s frame. Is this person legitimate? Are they comparable to the others on this site? || Matters most when your industry has a dominant directory or marketplace. Photographers have specific marketplaces. Therapists have Psychology Today. Home services have several. When the prospect starts inside the platform, you need to be in there. || Matters less when there’s no dominant marketplace. When prospects find you through Google or referral. When the platform’s framing dilutes what you do rather than helps it.

**11.** Touchpoint five: online communities. The in-the-room you. Different from social platforms, where you post out to an audience. Different from listings, where you’re reviewed. Communities are spaces where your prospects participate with each other. Industry Facebook groups. The local town and neighborhood groups where people ask for recommendations. Reddit threads in your category. Niche Discord servers. Forums that pre-date social media and still hold the real conversations. You show up as a peer, not a brand. You answer questions, you share thinking, you let the work speak by being present. Over time, people recognize you, and that recognition becomes trust, then inquiries. This || matters most when your prospects gather in communities you can identify, and when the buying decision involves long peer research, the kind where people ask their group before they search. It || matters less when they don’t gather online, when discovery is search or map based, or when being genuinely present would take more time than you can give. A neglected community presence is worse than none at all.

**12.** Second principle. And the more recent one. Your touchpoints aren’t just being read by people anymore. They’re also being read by LLMs. ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity. The systems that synthesize answers when someone asks “best wedding photographer in Brooklyn” or “find me a therapist who works with X.” Here’s the strategic observation. LLMs aren’t a sixth touchpoint. They read the same five. Your website. Your Google listing. Your social. Your third-party presence. Your community participation. They pull from all of it and synthesize an answer. Which means the work of being visible to LLMs is mostly the work you’re already doing for humans. Be clear. Be specific. Be named consistently across every touchpoint where you appear. Same name, same specialty, same location, said the same way wherever you show up. The work doesn’t change. The audience just got bigger.

The primary touchpoint carries the persuasion, the proof, and the full case for working with you. The secondaries can stay light. If your audit shows you don’t have most of these yet, that’s fine. The framework still applies. Build your primary first. The others can wait until the primary is doing its job. Build the primary so it holds everything it has to. Let the others do less.

**23.** Another principle. More presence is not better presence. Two touchpoints maintained well do more for your business than five touchpoints maintained badly. Concentration beats spread, in this work. A dead Instagram with three posts from 2022 says something specific about your business. Often something you didn’t intend. Match presence to capacity. If you can only do two touchpoints well, only have two touchpoints.

**24.** Once you know your primary touchpoint, the next question is: what does strong look like for that touchpoint? Take a website as the example. Weak says: it exists. The site is technically up. The positioning is generic. The proof is missing or thin. The pricing isn’t stated. The next step isn’t obvious. The visitor reads it and leaves to look at someone else’s site. || Strong says: it does the job. The positioning is specific enough that two competitors couldn’t have written the same about page. The proof is current. The pricing or process is named. The next step is one click. The visitor either books or self-selects out. Both of those are wins. The pattern transfers to other touchpoints. A weak Google listing has the basic facts. A strong one has the basic facts plus active review responses and current photos. A weak Instagram is sporadic and inconsistent. A strong one is a clear, narrow point of view, even if posts are infrequent. The threshold for strong isn’t being polished. It’s doing the touchpoint’s specific job.

**25.** Third principle. The buyer decides which touchpoint matters. By their behavior, not by your wish. This is the principle that costs people the most. Many service businesses have a touchpoint they love working on, and a different one their clients actually use. If you love producing Instagram content but every client comes through Google, search matters more than social, no matter what you prefer. The audit’s discovery channel question caught this. Where do clients actually first hear about you? That’s where you build. You can love your secondary touchpoint. You can keep it alive. But primary is determined by buyer behavior, not by your wish. Build where they are.

**26.** Now do the work, right here on this slide. You’ll see five touchpoint options: website, search and map, social, third-party, and online communities. Mark which ones apply to your business, then drag to set the priority order. Your audit answers are the guide. Use them. Don’t pick your favorite touchpoint. Pick what’s already working in your business. This isn’t where you decide what to build. This is where you describe what’s already true. Module 8 is where everything you’ve set across the course pulls together into one plan.

**27.** End of Module 3. You know where you get found. You know which touchpoints are primary for your business and which are secondary. You know what each one is supposed to do. And if your buyers are local, you know what a strong local presence actually looks like. Next module is the natural follow-on. Once a prospect finds you, what do they meet, and does it earn their trust? That’s Earned Proof. Module 4. Take a break if you need one. Module 4 begins when you’re ready. ## Module 4: Earned Proof

**1.** Welcome to Module 4. Earned proof. This is the second of the foundation modules. In Module 3 you worked out where you exist online, your touchpoints, and which one is primary. This module is about something that lives inside those places. Not what you say there. What other people say there. Twenty minutes. By the end you’ll have an inventory of your earned proof, you’ll know which of three sources your business should lean on, and you’ll know where your gaps are. Let’s start with why this is hard to do.

**2.** Here’s the thing most courses skip past. For a lot of people, the whole topic of reviews and testimonials and proof comes with a knot in the stomach. You finish good work for someone. You’d like them to leave a review, or write a few sentences you could use. And asking for that feels like begging. Like you’re imposing. Like you’re admitting you need something. So you don’t ask. The work was good, the client was happy, and the proof never gets created. I’m naming this at the start because if we don’t deal with the discomfort, none of the rest of the module gets used. We’ll come back to it directly. For now, just know the feeling is normal, and it’s based on a misunderstanding we’re going to clear up.

**3.** Here’s the concept the whole module rests on. When you tell a prospect you’re good at what you do, they hear it, and they quietly discount it. Not because they think you’re lying. Because you’re the one selling. Everyone discounts the seller’s account of themselves. You do it too, every time you shop. What || a prospect can’t discount as easily is evidence that didn’t come from you. A stranger’s review. A past client’s words. A visible before-and-after. Another professional sending someone your way. A credential from a body with authority. You didn’t author any of that, and that’s exactly why it carries weight. That’s earned proof. Evidence of your work that comes from outside you. Reviews are one kind of it. They are not the whole thing, and treating them as the whole thing is the first mistake to avoid.

**4.** So if earned proof is bigger than reviews, what is the whole of it? It comes from three places. Three different voices that can vouch for you. The next slide lays them out, and then we’ll take each one in turn.

**5.** Three sources of earned proof. They’re organized by who is doing the vouching. Source one: client proof. Your clients vouch for you. Reviews, testimonials, case studies, before-and-afters. It answers the question in the prospect’s head: did people like me get what they wanted? Source two: peer proof. Other professionals || vouch for you. Referrals and endorsements from experts whose own judgment a prospect already trusts. It answers a different question: do people who would actually know rate this person? Source three: institutional proof. An outside || body vouches for you. Credentials, licenses, certifications, awards, press, association membership. It answers: has something with real authority verified this person? || Most businesses lean on one of these as their primary source. Which one depends on the business. Hold that in mind. We’ll go through all three, and then you’ll see five businesses land in five different places.

**6.** Source one in detail. Client proof. This is the one everyone thinks of first. Reviews and star ratings on platforms. Testimonials in the client’s own words. Case studies that walk through a piece of work. Before-and-afters. Anything where the person you served is the one vouching. It matters most when your || work is comparable, when a prospect is weighing you against alternatives and a body of client proof tips the decision. When the buying decision is quick and a star rating settles it. And how recent the proof is matters here more than anywhere: a recent review signals you’re still good now, not just that you were good once. And it’s not just the reviews themselves. An owner who responds to them, the good ones and the critical ones, signals something a star rating can’t. Handling a hard review well, publicly and without defensiveness, often builds more trust than the praise does. A prospect reads it as: this is someone who shows up even when it’s uncomfortable. It matters || less in three cases. When professional ethics restrict you from soliciting or displaying client testimony, which is real for therapists, lawyers, some healthcare. When the work is so bespoke that no two clients are really comparable. And when, in your field, a client’s opinion carries less weight than an expert’s would.

**7.** Source two. Peer proof. This is the one that gets overlooked, and for some businesses it’s the most important of the three. Peer proof is when other professionals vouch for you. A referral from an adjacent expert. An endorsement from someone whose own professional judgment a prospect already trusts. The therapist a doctor sends patients to. The photographer a wedding planner recommends. The contractor an architect names. Why does it carry so much weight? Because the person vouching has their own reputation on the line. When a client says you’re good, that’s one happy customer. When another professional refers someone to you, they’re staking their own credibility on you being good. A prospect knows that, and weighs it accordingly. It matters || most when the work is high-stakes and a prospect wants a name that’s already been vetted. When client proof is restricted, peer proof is often the strongest channel still open. And it matters most when the referring expert has a real reputation to protect. It matters less when the purchase || is low-stakes and quick, or when your field simply has no natural network of adjacent professionals to refer across. One boundary worth naming. We’re talking about peer referral here as proof, as evidence that you’re credible. Actually building and maintaining referral relationships as a system is its own foundation, and it’s the next module. Here, we’re just establishing that peer endorsement is proof, and that yours should be visible.

**8.** Source three. Institutional proof. This is when an outside body vouches for you. Credentials. Licenses. Certifications. Awards, the ones that actually mean something. Press in an outlet your prospects respect. Membership in a professional body that genuinely vets the people it admits. It matters || most when your field requires verification and a prospect expects to see it. When the work involves trust, risk, or regulation, a license isn’t a nice-to-have, it’s the entry ticket to being considered at all. Nobody hires an unlicensed therapist because the website looked nice. It matters less || when the work has no formal credentialing and none is expected, or when prospects judge purely by results they can see for themselves. For a vivids stylist, the before-and-after is the proof. A certificate on the wall changes very little.

**9.** A principle, and it’s the bright line of this whole module. Earned proof reflects something real that actually happened. Manufactured proof simulates it. Fake reviews. Testimonials traded for a discount. Referral rings where professionals agree to send each other business regardless of fit. Awards you were invited to pay to receive. All of these borrow the look of proof without the thing proof is supposed to evidence, which is real, satisfied, voluntary recognition of good work. Two reasons not to go near manufactured proof. The honest one: it’s a lie, and the brand you’re building is supposed to be the opposite of that. The practical one: a prospect can usually tell, eventually, and the cost of being caught is the exact credibility you were trying to build. Manufactured proof is a loan against your reputation, at a terrible rate.

**10.** Now back to the discomfort we opened with. The feeling that asking for proof is begging. Here’s the reframe. A client who is genuinely glad they hired you often wants to say so, and has no natural occasion to. The work ended, life moved on. They’re not going to write an unprompted review three weeks later. When you ask, you’re not extracting a favor from them. You’re handing them a door they were already standing near. You’re giving the goodwill somewhere to go. If the work was good and the client was happy, asking is a small, ordinary thing. If the work wasn’t good, the discomfort you feel isn’t about asking, it’s about the work, and that’s a different problem to solve. And with peers, asking is even further from begging. A referral relationship between two professionals who respect each other’s work is just ordinary reciprocity. It’s the normal traffic of people who are good at adjacent things. You’re not asking for charity. You’re offering a connection that helps their clients too.

**11.** So you’ve got three sources. Client, peer, institutional. The question is which one is primary for you. Like everything in this course, the answer depends on your business, and your audit already pointed at it. Let’s watch five businesses work it out.

**12.** Same three-source framework, five businesses, and the answers genuinely diverge. Maya, the therapist. Her audit flagged an ethics-regulated profession, and that changes everything here. Professional ethics codes largely close client reviews and testimonials to her. So her earned proof comes from the other two sources. Peer proof: referrals from doctors, psychiatrists, other therapists. Institutional proof: her license and credentials. For Maya, the source most people build first is the one she mostly can’t use. That’s not a problem, it just means her inventory looks different. Marcus, || the pressure washer. The opposite case. Client proof is nearly his whole apparatus. Google reviews, lots of them, kept recent, decide whether he gets the job. Referrals from realtors and contractors are a real second source. Institutional proof barely matters, nobody asks a pressure washer for credentials. Lena, the wedding photographer. She pulls || on all three. Client testimonials and her portfolio. Referrals from planners and venues. Features in wedding press. A wedding is a high-trust, once-only purchase, so a prospect reaches for every kind of proof they can find. James, the chiropractor. Also three-way. || Reviews bring him volume. Referrals from GPs and physiotherapists bring vetted trust. And his state license clears the entry bar that a health practitioner has to clear before anything else matters. Sasha, the vivids || stylist. Client proof again, but a specific form of it: before-and-afters. The photo is the proof. Other stylists who don’t do vivid color send those clients to her, so peer proof is a real second. Institutional proof is close to irrelevant in her world. Same framework. Five different answers. Yours will be a sixth.

**13.** A principle about maintenance, because earned proof is not a thing you build once and forget. Proof ages. A glowing testimonial from four years ago raises a quiet question it can’t answer: what about since then? Is this person still good? Did something change? And it ages at different rates by source. Client proof ages fastest, because recency is itself a signal, a review from last month is worth more than the same review from 2020. Peer proof ages more slowly, but a referral relationship that’s gone quiet is a weakening one. Institutional proof ages slowest, a credential mostly holds, though some need renewal. The practical takeaway: when you do your inventory in a few minutes, you’re not just looking for missing proof. You’re looking for stale proof. Old proof and no proof are two different problems, and both need attention.

**14.** A principle about restraint. You saw the parallel in Module 3, where more presence wasn’t better presence. The same logic applies here. More proof is not better proof. Forty thin, generic reviews do less work than six specific ones. A wall of client logos does less than a single case study a prospect can see themselves in. Volume is not the goal. The question is never how much proof you can accumulate. It’s whether the proof you put in front of a prospect answers the actual doubt in their head. A prospect worried about reliability needs different proof than one worried about skill. Match the proof to the question. That’s the whole discipline.

**15.** Let’s make more proof is not better proof concrete. Two testimonials. Same client, imagine, just phrased two ways. The weak one: amazing to work with, highly recommend. Read it honestly. It could be about anyone, in any profession, doing any kind of work. It names no problem, no result, no specifics. A prospect reads it and learns nothing they can actually use. It fills a space on the page without doing a job. The || strong one: we came in after a failed rebrand, she found the positioning we’d been circling for a year, in three sessions. That names the starting problem, the result, and the shape of the work. A prospect who is sitting in that exact situation, a failed rebrand, circling their positioning, reads that and sees themselves. That recognition is what proof is for. Notice this is free. The strong testimonial didn’t cost more than the weak one. It just came from asking better, which is something the tool and the next module will help you do.

**16.** A short principle, and it connects this module back to the last one. Proof in a folder does nothing. Earned proof only works at the point where a prospect actually meets it. A testimonial saved in a document on your desktop is not proof. It’s a note to yourself. Remember your primary touchpoint from Module 3, the one place that carries the most weight in a prospect’s journey. That’s where your strongest proof belongs, positioned at the moment a prospect is deciding. Build the proof, then put it where the doubt actually is. We’re not re-teaching touchpoints here, just connecting the two: Module 3 told you where, this module tells you what goes there.

**17.** Before the tool, one thing for anyone whose honest reaction to all this is: I don’t really have any of it. If your inventory comes up nearly empty, you are not behind. You’re where most service businesses are early on, and where every business is right after a pivot. Thin proof is a starting point, not a failure, and the worst response to it is trying to build all three sources at once. || Find the one source most open to you right now. For most businesses that’s client proof from recent work, the people you’ve served in the last year, asked properly, while the work is still fresh in their minds. One source built properly beats three left thin. You don’t need a full apparatus by Friday. You need one good piece, then the next.

**18.** The principle that ties the module together. A prospect believes what they can verify without you in the room. Everything you say about yourself is heard with you present, and discounted for exactly that reason. Earned proof is different because it gets checked when you’re not there. A review read at midnight. A referral given over coffee when you’ll never know it happened. A credential confirmed in a ten-second search. That’s not a weakness of earned proof. It’s the entire point of it. It survives your absence. It works in the rooms you’ll never be in. That’s why building it is worth the small discomfort of asking.

**19.** Now your turn, right here on this slide. Set your Earned Proof strategy. You’ll see three columns, one for each source: client, peer, institutional. Go across them and mark three things. What you already have. What you have but has gone stale. And where the gaps are, the proof that fits your business but doesn’t exist yet. Then do the one thing that matters most: name the single source to build first. Use your audit. Use the five examples. If you’re not sure, and you have served clients recently, start with client proof. Everything you set here pulls together with the rest in Module 8. Take five minutes. Be honest about what’s stale. Then come back for the close.

**20.** That’s Module 4. You started this module with a knot in your stomach about asking. You’re ending it with an inventory: you know what earned proof you have, which of the three sources is yours to build, and where the gaps are. You know proof has to be real, current, specific, and placed where the doubt is. One source kept coming up as proof: peer referral. The fact that other professionals send people to you. In this module that was evidence. In the next module it becomes a system, something you build and maintain on purpose rather than hope for. That’s Module 5, the Referral System. I’ll see you there.

## Module 5: Referral System

**1.** This is Module 5, Referral System. It is the fourth of the seven foundations, and it sits in the second layer of the course, Get Chosen. Module 4 was about earned proof: the evidence that other people vouch for you. One of the sources it named was peer referral, the fact that other professionals send work your way. Module 4 treated that as proof. It is proof. But proof is a fact about your credibility, and this module asks a different question. Not is a referral proof, but can you cause more of them. That is the shift. Module 4 looked at the referral as a signal. Module 5 looks at it as a system. By the end you will have one output, your referral map, and a clear view of the single thing in your referral loop most worth fixing.

**2.** Here is where most people start with referrals. They feel like luck. A good one arrives, and it feels like weather: welcome, and entirely outside your control. You cannot make it rain. That belief is worth saying out loud, because it quietly shapes behavior. If referrals are luck, there is nothing to do about them, so most people do nothing, and then the lack of referrals confirms the belief. It is a closed circle. This module is here to open that circle. Not with a trick, and not by promising you can force referrals. By showing you that a referral is an event with causes, and that causes can be worked.

**3.** Think about the last good referral you received. It probably felt like a single moment: someone mentioned you, a stranger got in touch. But that moment had a history behind it. Something you did, possibly months earlier, gave that person a reason to refer you. Something made it natural for them to bring you up. Something, or nothing, happened afterward that shaped whether they would do it again. || Those are causes. They are quiet, and they are spread out over weeks or months, which is exactly why the referral feels like luck. You cannot see the chain, so you assume there is no chain. There is a chain. The rest of this module is that chain, broken into three stages you can look at one at a time. Once you can see it, a referral stops being weather and becomes a system: something with parts, and parts can be built, and parts can be fixed.

**4.** Here is the map for the rest of the module. A referral runs in three stages, and each stage has one condition that has to hold for the referral to make it through. That structure is what makes the loop useful, not just tidy. When referrals are not coming, the loop tells you it is not a vague problem with your whole business. It is one stage. A referral fails at a specific point, and once you know the three points, you can tell which one is yours.

**5.** Three stages. Earn, Ask, Keep. Earn is the first. A referral needs something to carry, and that something is a specific, nameable result. Not a happy client, exactly, but a client who can say what you did and who it was for. We will see why the word specific is doing all the work there. || Ask is the second. At some point the referral has to become possible: the other person has to know you welcome referrals, and has to know who you are the right call for. The good news, and we will come back to this, is that if asking has ever felt like begging, that is usually a sign the ask had no shape, not a sign that asking is wrong. || Keep is the third. After a referral happens, something either keeps that relationship alive or lets it go quiet. Keep is the work of keeping it alive, so the person who referred you would happily do it again. And here is the part that makes it a loop rather than a checklist. || Keep feeds back into Earn. A referral you handled well does not just bring one client. It tells the person who referred you that referring you works, and that makes the next one more likely. The third stage refills the first. That is the whole shape: three stages, running in a circle, slowly.

**6.** Earn. The first stage, and the one most people skip past, because it does not look like referral work. It looks like just doing the job. But there is a difference between a client being satisfied and a client being able to refer you. Satisfaction is a feeling. A referral needs a sentence. || The client has to be able to say what you did, in words, to a third person who is not in the room. If the best they can manage is she was great, that sentence carries nothing, and it does not travel. So Earn is not only about doing good work. It is about doing work that is legible to the client: work where they can see and name what changed. Sometimes that means simply telling them, plainly, what you did and why, so they have the language. For a peer it is the same requirement in a different key. || A peer is not referring you off a feeling. They are staking a piece of their own professional reputation on you, and they will only do that when they have clearly seen the kind of work you are the right call for. Earn, for a peer, is letting them see your judgment closely enough to trust it.

**7.** Ask. This is the stage people dread, so let us deal with the dread directly. Asking for a referral often feels like begging. Like you are standing there with your hand out, asking someone for a favor they did not offer. If that is how it feels, I want to suggest the problem is not that you are asking. The problem is that the ask has no shape. A shapeless ask sounds like: if you know anyone who needs my services, send them my way. That asks the other person to do work. They have to scan everyone they know against a vague category, and they will not, because it is a chore. That version does feel like begging, because it is all request and no help. || A shaped ask is different. It names a specific person and a specific moment: if you ever meet someone circling the same decision you were, that is exactly who I am for. Now you have not handed them a chore. You have handed them a signal to watch for. You have made it easy. That is not a favor extracted. It is closer to a gift, because you have given them a way to help someone they care about. With peers, the ask is even plainer. || Between two professionals who respect each other, routing work back and forth is not an imposition. It is ordinary reciprocity, the normal traffic of people who trust each other’s judgment. The ask there is just making that traffic explicit.

**8.** Keep. The third stage, and the one most quietly neglected, because by the time it matters the referral has already happened and it feels like the work is done. It is not done. Picture it from the referrer’s side. They mentioned you to someone. They took a small social risk: their judgment was on the line, in a small way, the moment they said your name. And then, often, nothing comes back to them. They never learn whether it helped, whether you were a good fit, whether it mattered at all. It vanishes into silence. Silence is not neutral. Silence teaches. It teaches the referrer that referring you leads nowhere they can see, and so, without any bad feeling, they stop. Keep is the work of not letting that happen. || For a client, keeping is usually small and human: a genuine, specific thank-you. Not a reward, just an acknowledgement that you noticed the risk they took and you are grateful for it. That is what makes a second referral feel natural rather than used up. || For a peer, keeping is staying in real professional contact between referrals, and returning the traffic when you can. A peer relationship is kept, not transacted. It stays warm because both people keep showing up, not because anyone is keeping score. And whether it is a client or a peer, staying in genuine contact is easier when you have a reliable way to reach them. For many businesses that is exactly what an owned channel is for, which is Module 7. And remember the loop. A referral that is kept well does not just preserve one relationship. It makes that person more likely to refer you again. Keep is the stage that feeds Earn. It is the reason this is a loop.

**9.** Before we work out which stage is yours, one principle, because the referral world is full of advice that will quietly damage the thing you are trying to build. A referral works because it is a judgment. One person tells another the truth as they honestly see it, and the second person trusts it precisely because nothing rides on it. That trust is the entire value. The moment you attach a fee, a commission, a trade, a discount for sending people, the judgment bends. Even slightly. The referrer now has a reason to refer you that is not their honest read of whether you are the right fit. And prospects can feel that bend, often without naming it. A referral that is really a sales commission does not carry the same weight, because somewhere the recipient senses it is not purely a judgment anymore. In some fields this is not only a matter of trust, it is a matter of rules. Several regulated professions forbid paying for referrals outright, and the reasoning behind those rules is exactly this: a paid referral is a compromised judgment. Now, hear the line clearly, because Keep involves thanking people and I do not want that confused with paying them. Acknowledgement is not payment. A genuine, specific thank-you rewards the person for the risk they took without renting their opinion. The test is simple. Does the thing you are giving create a reason to refer you that is not honest judgment? A thank-you does not. A finder’s fee does. Keep the judgment clean, and the referral keeps its worth.

**10.** One more principle before the calibration, and it is the one that answers the luck belief directly. A referral system does not pay off the way an ad does. There is no spike. It pays slowly, and then, if you keep the loop running, steadily. Here is why. Each referral you keep well does two things at once. It brings you a client now, and it teaches the referrer that referring you actually works. That second effect is the loop closing, and it is invisible in the moment. This is the real reason referrals feel like luck. It is not that they have no cause. It is that the loop runs too slowly to watch. The cause and the effect are weeks or months apart, so your eye cannot connect them, and your mind fills the gap with the word luck. Build the loop on purpose and nothing about that slowness changes. But its meaning changes. The same slow, hard-to-see process that felt like randomness becomes something else: reliability. A referral system is slow the way compound interest is slow. That is not its weakness. Given a little patience, it is the whole point.

**11.** So here is the question the rest of the module turns on. The loop has three stages. Which one is yours to fix? Almost no business is weak at all three. If you were weak at all three you would have no referrals at all, and most businesses have at least some. What is far more common is that the loop is running, but leaking. Strong at one or two stages, and quietly losing referrals at one specific point. The next slide shows that pattern across five businesses, so you can find the shape of your own.

**12.** Five businesses. Same three-stage loop. But notice two things vary: where the referrals actually come from, and which stage leaks. Maya, the therapist. Almost every referral comes from another professional. Ethics rules close off client reviews for her, so doctors and other therapists are her referral channel. Her Earn is strong: peers respect her work. Her weak stage is Keep. Those peer relationships go quiet in the long gaps between referrals, and a quiet professional relationship slowly cools. Her work is to keep the peer network alive, not to ask harder. || Marcus, the pressure washer. Referrals come mostly from past clients, with some realtor and contractor partners alongside. His Earn is fine, the work is visibly good, and he would keep a relationship happily. His weak stage is Ask. He simply never mentions referrals. He assumes good work travels by itself, and so a lot of willing customers never get the small nudge that would turn them into referrers. || Lena, the wedding photographer. Referrals come from peers: planners and venues. She is comfortable asking; that stage is handled. Her weak stage is Earn, and this one is subtle. Her work is genuinely distinctive, a documentary style, but the planners referring her cannot name what makes it distinctive. So they refer her as just a photographer, generically, and the referral loses its edge. Her work is at the Earn stage: making her specialty nameable to the people who refer her. || James, the chiropractor. He has both: GP and physio referrals, plus patient word of mouth. His weak stage is Ask, in a specific way. He has never told the referring GPs which patients he is the right call for, so they send him a scattered mix and the fit is hit or miss. His work is to make the ask carry the fit. || Sasha, the vivids stylist. Referrals come from past clients, plus overflow from other stylists who do not do vivid color. Her Earn is vivid, literally, the before-and-afters sell themselves. Her weak stage is Keep. She never circles back to the stylists who send her work, so that overflow arrives sporadically instead of steadily. Her work is to keep the stylist relationships alive. Five businesses, and between them every stage shows up as the weak one. Hold your own business against these. You are looking for the same thing: not a business that is broken everywhere, but a loop that is leaking at one point.

**13.** A few principles now to settle the loop before you use the tool. The first is about the order of the stages, because the order is not arbitrary. It is tempting to treat referrals as purely an asking problem. Asking is the visible stage, the one that feels like a skill, the one most referral advice fixates on. But Earn comes first for a reason. If a client genuinely cannot name what you did for them, no improvement to your ask will fix it. A better-worded request aimed at a client with nothing specific to say just produces a more enthusiastic version of she was great. You cannot ask your way out of an Earn problem. The ask is a vehicle, and it can only carry what the work already made nameable. If the cupboard is empty, a better basket does not help.

**14.** The second principle is about fit, and it corrects a natural assumption: that any referral is a good referral. It is not. A referral pointed at the wrong person costs you more than no referral at all. Think about what a bad-fit referral actually does. A willing referrer, who does not really know who you are right for, sends you someone you cannot genuinely help. You spend a consultation discovering that. The prospect spends their time discovering it too. And worst of all, quietly, the referrer learns that referring you did not work, because the person they sent did not become a client. So a bad-fit referral burns a consultation, burns goodwill, and damages the very loop you are trying to build. This is why the ask cannot just be a name. It has to carry the fit. The referrer needs to know not only that you do this work, but who, specifically, you are the right call for. A good referral is aimed, not just sent.

**15.** Let us make the ask concrete, because it is the stage people most want to get right and most often get wrong. Two versions of the same ask, from a photographer. The weak version: if you know anyone who needs my services, send them my way. Read it the way the referrer hears it. It names no person and no problem. It asks them to hold a vague category in their head and match strangers against it, indefinitely. That is work, so they will not do it. And notice the shape of it: it is pure request. It sounds like a favor being asked, and that is exactly the thing that makes asking feel like begging. || The strong version: if a friend ever mentions they are dreading their family photos, that is exactly who I am for, feel free to point them to me. Look at what changed. It names a specific person, a friend, and a specific moment, the offhand comment about dreading photos. The referrer no longer has a chore. They have a signal to recognize, and recognition is easy, it happens by itself when the moment arrives. And the strong version does not feel like begging, because it is not pure request. It hands the referrer something useful: a way to help a friend who is quietly dreading something. That is the whole move. A good ask is specific enough to be a gift rather than a chore. Whatever your weak stage turns out to be, when you do ask, ask like the second version.

**16.** The third principle is about pace, and it matters because you have limited hours and this module must not ask for hours you do not have. A referral system has no launch week. There is no campaign, no push, no sprint. It runs at the speed of the relationships it is made of, and relationships move slowly. That is not a flaw in the system. It is the nature of the material. Which means the work is not large. It is small, and recurring. One honest ask, shaped well, when the moment is right. One specific thank-you when someone refers you. One peer relationship kept warm with a little genuine contact. None of that takes hours. It takes attention, applied consistently, in small amounts. A referral system is, in the end, a habit wearing the costume of a strategy. If you are picturing a project with a deadline, picture instead a small set of things you simply do, as a matter of course, from now on. That is the realistic shape of it, and it is the shape that fits inside a working week.

**17.** If the loop is starting to feel like a lot, here is the relief. You do not rebuild the whole loop. Look back at the five businesses: not one of them was weak at all three stages. Each had a single leak. If referrals already reach you, even occasionally, your loop is running, and quietly leaking at one point. So the work is focused. Pick one referral source you already have. Not a hypothetical one, a real one: a particular kind of client, or a specific peer or type of peer who sends you work, or could. Just one. || Then walk that one source through the three stages. For that source, is Earn solid: do they have something specific to say about you? Is Ask solid: do they know you welcome referrals and who you are right for? Is Keep solid: does the relationship stay alive after a referral? Somewhere in those three, for that source, you will find the leak. One stage, repaired properly, restores the whole loop. One honest caveat before the next slide. That whole instruction assumes referrals already reach you, that there is a loop to diagnose. If that is not true for you yet, you are not behind, and the next slide is yours.

**18.** This slide is for a specific buyer: the one who has just realized, honestly, that referrals do not really reach them yet. If that is you, the diagnostic on the last slide did not quite fit, because there is no running loop to find the leak in. That is not a failure. It is a normal place to start, and it has a clear first move. You might expect the first move to be asking. It is not. Think back to the principle a few slides ago: no ask repairs work that gave the client nothing to say. A referral has nothing to travel on until your work is nameable. If you start a referral system by asking, before the work is nameable, you are asking people to carry a sentence they do not have. The ask goes nowhere, and it confirms the feeling that referrals are not for you. || So a referral system, built from nothing, begins at Earn. Concretely: take one piece of your work, one project, one kind of result you produce, and make it specific enough that a client or a peer could describe it in a plain sentence. Not she was great. Something with a shape: what you did, and who it was for. That is the entire first move. One nameable piece of work. Earn first. Then, once there is something to carry, Ask becomes possible, and after that Keep. The three stages are the same three stages. You are not on a lesser path or a beginner version of the loop. You are building the same loop, in the order it has to be built. Ask and Keep are real, and they are coming. Earn is simply where a loop that does not exist yet has to start. When you reach the tool, this is the option to choose, and the tool will meet you there.

**19.** One last principle, and it connects this module back to the start of the course. Every referral happens in a conversation you are not in, and cannot influence in the moment. All the referrer carries into that conversation is the sentence they can say about you. That sentence is the entire referral. If it is clear, specific, easy to say, the referral is accurate and it travels well. If it is vague, something often worse than inaccuracy happens: the referral does not get made at all, because the referrer cannot find the words and the moment passes. So the clearer you are to describe, the more often you get described. And notice what that means. The work of being easy to refer is, underneath, the work of positioning, the very first foundation in this course. Module 1 asked you to be clear about who you are for and what you do. This is where that clarity pays a dividend you may not have expected: it is also what lets other people carry you accurately into rooms you will never enter. Referral and positioning are not two projects. They are the same work, met again.

**20.** Time to do the work, right here on this slide. Set your Referral System strategy. First, pick your one source. One real referral source you already have: a kind of client you have served, or a particular peer, or type of peer, who sends you work or plausibly could. Write that source at the top of the tab. Then walk it through the three stages. The tab has a row for each. For Earn, ask: does this source have something specific they could say about me, or only a vague good feeling? Mark it solid or leaking. For Ask: does this source actually know I welcome referrals, and know who I am the right call for? Solid or leaking. For Keep: after this source refers me, does anything keep that relationship alive, or does it go silent? Solid or leaking. You will usually find one stage clearly weaker than the other two. Name it. That is your weak stage. Then write one concrete change, a single specific thing you will do, to repair that stage for that source. Not a plan, one change. Everything you set across the course comes together into a full plan in Module 8, so it does not have to be finished today. It has to be honest and specific.

**21.** That is Module 5. A referral is no longer luck. It is a loop, with three stages, Earn, Ask, and Keep, and you have found the one stage most worth your attention and named one change to make to it. Step back and notice where the foundation stands. Everything you have built so far, getting found, earned proof, and now referrals, works on people who already know you, or who are sent to you by someone who does. That is a real and valuable foundation. But it has an edge. It reaches the people already connected to you. The next module reaches past that edge. Module 6 is Brand Awareness, and it is the first foundation in the third layer, Be Remembered. It is about becoming known by the people you want as clients before they are looking for you, and before anyone has referred you. Different work, and the natural next piece. I will see you in Module 6.

## Module 6: Brand Awareness

**1.** This is Module 6, Brand Awareness. It is the fifth of the seven foundations, and it opens the third and final layer of the course, Be Remembered. A quick word on where we are. The first layer, Get Found, was about being findable when someone goes looking. The second, Get Chosen, was about being credible and referable once they find you. This third layer is different. Be Remembered is about the people who are not looking yet, and have not been sent by anyone. It is the longest game in the course, and Brand Awareness is its foundation. By the end of this module you will have one output, your presence plan: one place to be known, and a cadence you can actually keep. Not a brand strategy in the abstract. One place, one rhythm, sized to the hours you really have.

**2.** Here is where most people start with brand awareness, and it is worth saying plainly because it shapes everything that follows. Building a brand feels infinite. Unlike the work in earlier modules, awareness seems to have no edges. There is always another platform, another post, another thing you could be doing. It never feels finished, because nothing about it tells you when you have done enough. And you are running this on five to ten hours a week. So the honest reaction to brand awareness is often a kind of quiet dread: a task too big to start, attached to time you do not have. That feeling is the first thing this module fixes. Not with more discipline, and not by asking you to find hours that are not there. By giving the work edges. Awareness only feels infinite because it has not been given a shape. The rest of this module is that shape.

**3.** So let us define the work precisely, because a lot of the dread comes from a blurred definition. Most marketing chases people who have a need right now. It tries to catch someone at the moment of searching. Awareness is a different kind of work. It is becoming familiar to the right people before they have the need at all, so that when the need does arrive, you are already the name that comes to mind. This is the single most important distinction in the module, so let me state it as bluntly as I can. Awareness is not lead generation. It will not produce an enquiry today. If you measure it by this week’s enquiries, it will look like failure every week. What it actually does is change who gets the enquiry later, when the need finally appears. Different job, different timescale. || One more piece of orientation. This is the first of the three Be Remembered foundations, and they have clean edges. Module 7, Owned Audience, is about the audience you can contact directly, on your terms. Module 8, Authority Building, is about being seen as the genuine expert in your field. Module 6, this one, sits underneath both. It is the plain groundwork: being known at all. You cannot have an audience or a reputation with people who have never heard of you.

**4.** Here is the model that gives awareness its shape. It is one line, and the whole module rests on it. Presence in one place, over time. Read the three parts. Presence: you actually show up, repeatedly, rather than existing as a profile that sits there. One place: a single defined place, not everywhere, and the word one is doing real work, because the belief that you must be everywhere is most of what made awareness feel infinite. Over time: it accumulates slowly, and the accumulation is the point. That is the cure for the infinite feeling. Awareness is not an endless field of possible activity. It is one place, returned to, for a long time. The next section makes that concrete: what makes a place the right place, and what presence actually requires to work.

**5.** Presence in a place over time builds awareness, but only when three conditions hold. Miss any one and the presence stops compounding, which means the hours you spend stop adding up to anything. The first condition is right place. The place has to be one where your future clients already gather. Presence is only valuable if the right people are there to register || it. The second is consistent. You have to show up on a rhythm, something close to predictable, not in occasional bursts. Awareness is an accumulation, and an accumulation needs repetition. || The third is recognizably you. Each time you appear, it has to read as the same person, with the same angle, so that the appearances connect to each other in a viewer’s mind. Repetition without recognizability is just noise that happens to come from one source. || Right place, consistent, recognizably you. The next three slides take each one in turn, because each has a specific failure mode worth seeing clearly.

**6.** Right place. The first condition, and the one that decides whether any of the effort is worth spending. Start with what a place even is. A place is anywhere the same group of people gathers, repeatedly. The right place, for you, is one your future clients already pass through. That word already matters. You are not trying to drag people somewhere new. You are positioning yourself where they reliably already are, so your presence reaches them as part of their ordinary week. || Now, an important correction, because this is where awareness work most often goes narrow. A place is not only a social media feed. It can be a local business network. A community organized around a profession next to yours. A niche online forum. A recurring event, in person or online. Even a newsletter that someone else already runs and would welcome your contribution. A social platform is one kind of place. For many service businesses it is not the best one. Choose the place by where your clients are, not by which platform is loudest. || And the failure mode. Presence in the wrong place builds awareness in an empty room. You can post consistently, sound exactly like yourself, do everything else right, and if your future clients are not in that room, none of it lands. Effort spent, no one who matters there to see it. The place is the first decision because getting it wrong makes the other two conditions irrelevant.

**7.** Consistent. The second condition, and the one that quietly decides whether awareness ever compounds. Awareness accumulates. It is never one appearance doing the work. It is the sum of many small ones, layered over time until a name becomes familiar. And accumulation has a requirement: repetition, on a rhythm steady enough that someone could almost predict it. || Here is the part that runs against instinct. A small, steady presence beats a big, rare one. One modest appearance every week will out-build an ambitious effort that shows up impressively and then disappears for two months. The rhythm itself is the asset. Not the size or polish of any single appearance, the fact of returning, reliably. A viewer’s sense of you is built by the pattern, not the peak. || The failure mode follows directly. A burst of effort followed by silence does not just pause the accumulation, it resets it. People forget at roughly the speed you stop. Which leads to the hard, useful conclusion of this whole condition: a consistency you cannot sustain is worse than a smaller one you can. An ambitious rhythm that breaks is not a strong start. It is a slow start that then un-builds itself. We will come back to this when you choose your cadence, because it is the single most common way awareness work fails.

**8.** Recognizably you. The third condition, and the one that turns mere repetition into actual recognition. You can be in the right place, showing up on a steady rhythm, and still build nothing, if each appearance looks like it came from a different person. For repetition to become recognition, the appearances have to connect. Same voice. Same angle. Same recognizable point of view, showing up again and again, so a viewer’s mind can link this week’s appearance to last month’s and form a single, stable impression of who you are. || And here is the relief in this condition, the part that should lower the workload rather than raise it. The angle that makes you recognizable is not something you invent for this module. It is the positioning you already defined back in Module 1. Awareness does not ask you to build a new identity. It asks you to take the one you already have and show it consistently. If you have done the earlier work, the hardest part of this condition is already finished. || The failure mode is presence with no through-line. If your presence changes shape every time, chasing whatever feels relevant that week, it reads as a different person each time. The appearances never link up. And then all that consistency, all that returning to the right place, adds up to noise instead of a name. Recognizability is the thread that strings the appearances into something a person can actually remember.

**9.** Before we work out how much awareness should matter for your particular business, one principle, because the awareness world is full of advice pointed at the wrong target. Almost all popular brand-building advice is really advice about reach. More followers. More views. A bigger audience, treated as the obvious goal. And it is worth being clear-eyed: that is not the same thing as awareness, and for a service business it is often a distraction dressed as progress. A thousand strangers who will never hire you are not awareness. They are an audience of the wrong people, and a large one does not become the right one by being large. What you are actually after is to be genuinely familiar to the specific, often quite small, group of people who could realistically become your clients. So a small room of the right people beats a crowded one. This matters practically, not just philosophically, because it shrinks the work. You are not trying to reach everyone. You are trying to be known by a defined few. That is a far smaller task, and a far more achievable one, than the reach-chasing version most advice will push you toward.

**10.** A second principle, and this is the one most likely to save your awareness practice from being abandoned. So I want to say it directly, before you have done a single week of the work. Of every foundation in this course, brand awareness is the hardest to watch working. Getting found can show a result in weeks. A referral system starts returning within a few cycles, and you can see it happen. Awareness is different: you will do the work, properly, and see nothing for a long while, not because it is failing, but because its progress is genuinely invisible while it forms. Here is why, so the silence does not unsettle you. Recognition forms quietly, below the surface. Someone sees you, then sees you again, and a faint familiarity starts to build, and none of that produces an enquiry or a signal you can measure. The work is genuinely invisible, right up until the moment it is not, when someone you have never spoken to gets in touch already knowing who you are. So expect the silence. Plan for it. And above all, do not read it as failure, because the natural thing to do at week six, seeing nothing, is to conclude it is not working and stop. The silence is not the absence of progress. It is what progress looks like, in this particular kind of work, for longer than feels comfortable. You will not see this one accumulate. That does not mean it isn’t.

**11.** Now the question this section turns on, and it is a different question from the one the earlier modules asked. In Module 5 the question was which stage is weak. Here it is something more basic: how much should awareness matter for you at all? Because awareness is not equally important for every business. For some, it is where almost all their business starts. For others, it is a low-priority supporting note, genuinely worth a little attention but not much. The next slide shows that range across the five businesses, and notice that two things vary: not just where each one’s place is, but how much weight awareness should carry for them in the first place.

**12.** Five businesses, the same model, presence in a place over time. But watch how much the weight varies, because this is the calibration that matters. Maya, the therapist. Awareness is low for her. She runs on peer referral, and that is genuinely where her business comes from. So her awareness work is light: a modest, professional presence in the local clinician community, enough that other practitioners remember she exists and what she specializes in. For Maya, small is not underdoing it. Small is correct. A large awareness push would pull hours away from the referral relationships that actually carry her practice. || Marcus, the pressure washer. Medium. Reviews are still where most of his business starts, but awareness genuinely helps him, and his place is specific: the local neighborhood groups, online and otherwise, where homeowners ask each other for recommendations. If he is already a familiar name when his service comes up there, every one of those conversations tilts his way. Worth real attention, but still not the centre. || Lena, the wedding photographer. High. Couples research for months before booking, which gives awareness time to do real work. Her place is the venue and planner community, plus a consistent presence showing her actual portfolio, so that her documentary style is recognized well before a couple ever reaches out. For Lena, awareness is close to high. || James, the chiropractor. Low, like Maya. He runs on referral and local search, and that is where his business comes from. Awareness is a small, steady note for him: a recognizable presence in one or two community spaces so the name is familiar locally. Useful, modest, and not the thing to overbuild. || Sasha, the vivids stylist. High. For Sasha, awareness is where all of it starts. Her clients discover her vivid color work on the platform where it lives, follow her for months, and book when they are ready. Presence is not a supporting note for Sasha. It is the business. Hold your own business against that range. Before you decide on a place or a cadence, decide honestly how much weight awareness should carry for you. If you are a Maya or a James, a small presence is the right answer and you should feel no guilt about it. If you are a Sasha, this is your main work. Most businesses sit somewhere in between, and naming where is the first real decision.

**13.** A few principles now to settle the model before you build your plan. The first follows straight from the calibration: build awareness to the size of its actual job, and no larger. If referral is where your business comes from, then awareness is a supporting note in your marketing, and a supporting note should be played quietly. This runs against a strong cultural pull. Everything around you suggests a bigger presence is always better, that more is always progress. It is not. Not every business needs a large presence, and for many service businesses a large one would be a mistake. Here is the real cost, the one that is easy to miss. Effort spent on awareness past what the job requires is not free. It is effort taken directly from what actually carries your business, your referrals, your proof, your local search. Overbuilding awareness does not just waste time. It quietly starves the thing that works. So match the presence to the weight you decided on in the calibration, and then, deliberately, stop. Stopping at the right size is a skill, and it is one this brand takes seriously.

**14.** The second principle is about capacity, and it is the most practical thing in the module, so I want it to be unmissable. The cadence you can actually keep beats the cadence you admire. Every time. An ambitious plan that you abandon after a month builds less awareness than a modest plan you hold steadily for a year. It is not close. Remember why, from the consistency condition. Recognition is forfeited the moment you stop. The accumulation resets. Which means an overreaching cadence is not just risky, it is, in a real sense, built to be stopped. You are designing in its own failure at the planning stage. So plan from your real capacity. Not the version of your week where everything goes smoothly and you have spare evenings. Your actual five to ten hours, with a hard week assumed, because hard weeks are not the exception, they are a regular feature of running a business alongside everything else. The test for your cadence is simple and a little uncomfortable: can you still keep this when the week goes wrong? If the honest answer is no, the cadence is too big, and you should choose a smaller one before you have built anything on top of it.

**15.** Let us make the plan concrete, because this is the stage where good intentions most often produce a plan that quietly cannot survive. Two versions. The weak plan: post daily on three platforms, start a newsletter, and attend every local event. It sounds committed. It sounds like someone taking awareness seriously. But read it against everything we have covered. It ignores capacity entirely. It spreads across many places instead of one. And the first genuinely hard week, the one with a sick child or a client crisis, ends it. Then comes the silence, and the silence un-builds the little that was built. This plan is not ambitious. It is fragile, and it is dressed up as ambition. || The strong plan: one thoughtful post a week, in the one community where my clients already are. That is it. One place. One rhythm. And notice your own reaction to it, because the reaction is the lesson. It looks too small. It looks like it cannot possibly be enough. But it satisfies every condition: it is one right place, it is a consistent rhythm, and crucially it is survivable, it can hold through a bad week without breaking. And held for a year, that small, almost embarrassing plan is exactly what recognition is made of. Fifty-some appearances, in the right place, all recognizably you. The weak plan probably produces six appearances and then nothing. When you build your own plan in a moment, build the one on the right. The one that looks slightly too small is almost always the correct one.

**16.** The third principle steps back, because it is worth understanding why this module is in the course at all, and why it sits where it does. Look at every foundation before this one. Getting found acts on people actively searching for what you offer. Earned proof acts on people already considering you. A referral system acts on people sent to you by someone who knows you. Every one of them, valuable as it is, works on people who are already near you in some way. Already searching, or already connected. Awareness is the first foundation that reaches past that edge. It works on people who are not looking yet, and who have not been sent by anyone, people entirely outside the circle of those who already know you exist. That is a genuinely harder reach, and it is the entire reason the Be Remembered layer exists. A foundation built only on people already near you has a ceiling, the size of that circle. Awareness is what lets the circle itself grow. It is also why awareness works by compounding. Each month of presence adds to the last, and the reach you build does not reset. So when the early work feels quiet, and it will, hold on to this: you are not waiting for nothing, you are accumulating.

**17.** If the plan is starting to feel like a lot, the instruction is almost the opposite of what the feeling wants. Start smaller than feels right. The instinct, especially if you feel behind, is to begin with an ambitious presence to make up for lost time. Resist that instinct completely. It is the single most reliable way this work fails. Remember the test from the capacity principle. An awareness practice has to survive your worst week, not your best one. Build it for the good weeks and it breaks on the first bad one, and the silence afterward un-builds the recognition you had managed to accumulate. So the plan is built around your worst week on purpose. || Concretely: pick one place, the single place from the calibration where your clients already are. Then pick the smallest cadence that still honestly counts as a rhythm. Weekly is good. Fortnightly can be enough. Here is a useful gut-check: choose the version you are slightly embarrassed is not more. That faint embarrassment is usually the sign you have got the size right. And this is reversible in only one direction. If your small plan holds for a year, you can always add to it. But if you start too big and it collapses, you cannot un-collapse it, you have to rebuild from a worse position than zero. Almost no one regrets starting too small. A great many people quit from starting too big. That is the work the tool sets up now.

**18.** One last principle before the tool, and it reframes what you are actually aiming for, because the word brand can make this sound grander and more effortful than it is. No one ever decides to know a business. Think about how you came to know the shops on a street you walk often. You did not study them. You passed them, day after day, and at some point their names were simply part of the landscape in your mind. That is how familiarity actually forms: through quiet, repeated exposure, not through any single announcement. Awareness works the same way. It is not a campaign that announces you to the world. It is a presence that, simply by being kept up in the right place, gradually becomes ordinary. Becomes part of the furniture of that particular room. And I want to end the teaching on that word, ordinary, because it sounds like a letdown and it is the opposite. Ordinary, in the right place, is exactly the goal. You are not trying to be a sensation. You are trying to become the familiar, unremarkable, obvious name that comes to mind when a specific need arises among a specific group of people. That is a calm, achievable, deeply effective aim. And it is well within reach of five to ten hours a week, if the place is right and the rhythm holds.

**19.** Time to build your plan, right here on this slide. Set your Brand Awareness strategy. First, before anything else, note how much weight awareness should carry for you. Low, medium, high, or not applicable. Be honest, using the calibration as your guide. That weighting governs everything that follows: it tells you how small your plan is allowed to be. Second, name your one place. A single, specific place where your future clients already are. Not a list of platforms, one place. Write it down. Third, check that place against the three conditions. Is it genuinely the right place, where your clients actually gather? Can you realistically be consistent there? Can you show up there recognizably as yourself? If a condition does not hold, the place is wrong, and it is far better to find that out now, on the page, than after six months of effort. Fourth, commit to a cadence, and here is the instruction I want you to take literally: deliberately under-commit. Pick the rhythm you could keep through a genuinely bad week, not a good one. If you are torn between two cadences, choose the smaller one. Everything you set across the course comes together into a full plan in Module 8, so it does not have to be ambitious today. It has to be honest, specific, and survivable.

**20.** That is Module 6. Brand awareness is no longer an infinite, edgeless task. It has a shape: presence, in one place, over time. You know the three conditions that make presence work, you have weighed how much it should matter for your business, and you have a presence plan built small enough to actually survive. One thing to carry into the next module. Awareness, as we have built it, makes you familiar, but notice where it happens: in a place, and that place usually belongs to someone else. A platform, a community, a forum. You are building recognition on borrowed ground, ground whose rules you do not set. That is what Module 7 addresses. Owned Audience is about turning the presence you have just planned into an audience you can reach directly, on your own terms, whenever you choose, without depending on someone else’s platform to carry your message. It is the natural next step: first you become known in a place, then you build a way to reach the people who came to know you. I will see you in Module 7.

## Module 7: Owned Audience

**1.** This is Module 7, Owned Audience. It is the sixth of the seven foundations, and the second module in the third layer of the course, Be Remembered. Module 6 was Brand Awareness. It was about becoming known, building a presence in one place so the right people encounter you. This module picks up exactly where that one ended. Module 6 closed on a specific point: the place you became known is borrowed. It is a platform you do not own, and its reach is held at someone else’s discretion. Owned Audience is the answer to that. It is about building a direct line to the people who came to know you, a line you control, one that does not vanish when a platform changes its rules. By the end you will have one output, your owned-audience plan: the people, the channel, and the cadence you can actually sustain.

**2.** Here is where most established businesses start with the idea of an owned audience. Not curious. Already decided against it. And the decision usually sounds like one of three things. The first: I tried this. I started a newsletter, sent a few, nobody opened them, and I quietly stopped. The second: I do not really need this. My clients come from referrals and word of mouth, and after the last two modules, that is working. The third: that whole audience-building thing is not me. It is what influencers do, and I am not an influencer. I want to be straight with you. Every one of those is a reasonable response. But notice what they are all responding to: a particular, loud, hype-heavy version of audience-building. The aggressive list growth, the daily emails, the funnel. You are right to reject that. The problem is that the real thing got thrown out with it. You judged owned audience by what reach-chasing looks like, and they are not the same thing. This module is about the real thing.

**3.** So let me name the distinction the whole module turns on. There is reach, and there is a channel you own. They are two different things, and most businesses only have the first. Reach is what Module 6 built. You established a presence somewhere, and a platform shows your work to people. That reach is genuine. It is also borrowed. The platform stands between you and your audience, and it decides, every single time, who actually sees you. It can change that decision overnight, for reasons it does not explain, and you would have no recourse. || A channel you own is the other thing. It is a direct line to people who came to know you and chose to keep hearing from you. Email is the clearest example. When you have someone’s address and their permission, no platform is in the middle. You can reach that person when you decide to, not when an algorithm allows it. This module lives in the gap between those two. Reach is borrowed. A channel you own is yours. And only one of them can be a foundation.

**4.** Here is the move this section makes. It is a shift from one verb to another: from being found to being reachable. Those sound similar and they are not. Being found is something other people do to you, when a platform decides to show you to them. Being reachable is something you can do, whenever you choose, because you hold the line yourself. Module 6 left you findable. This module is about becoming reachable, and reachable does not happen by accident. It is built, on purpose, and the rest of this section is how.

**5.** Let us be precise about the two jobs, because the mistake this module corrects is a mix-up between them. Reach has one job: it finds people. A platform takes your work and puts it in front of people who do not know you yet. That is valuable, and an owned channel does not replace it. First contact is reach’s job, and it does it well. But the platform owns that connection. You are, in a real sense, renting access to your own audience. || A channel you own has the other job: it keeps people. It is a direct line to people who already know you and have chosen to stay in contact. Nothing stands between you and them. || Now here is the mistake. People pour their effort into reach and treat the follower count as if it were an audience they have. It is not. It is an audience the platform has, and lets you borrow. Trying to own reach is chasing something that, by its nature, cannot be owned. The honest move is to let reach do its job, finding people, and then build the channel that does the other job, keeping them. You are not choosing between the two. You are making sure you actually have the second one.

**6.** Let us look at borrowed reach honestly, up close, because the point is not that it is bad. It is that it is limited in one specific way. What reach does well: it introduces you. A platform puts your work in front of people who were genuinely never going to find you on their own. That is real, and for that job, first contact, nothing an owned channel does competes with it. This is why Module 6 mattered and why reach is worth having. || What reach cannot do: it cannot promise you a second contact. This is the limit. You can reach a person today through a platform, and have no reliable way to reach that same person next month. The platform decides every time, independently, and it does not consult you. There is no continuity you control. || And here is why that matters. A business that has only reach has to be re-found, over and over, by the same people. Every conversation starts from zero, because nothing carries forward. You can run a business that way. Many do. It is workable, and it is quietly exhausting, and it is not a foundation, because a foundation is the thing you build on, and you cannot build on a connection you have to re-establish every time.

**7.** One principle here, because it is the hinge of the whole module. In Module 6 you built a presence in one place. That was the right work. But that place is borrowed ground. It is a platform you do not own, running on rules you did not set, rules you often cannot even see, and the company behind it can change them whenever it serves the company. Your reach there is genuine, and it sits entirely at someone else’s discretion. Hear what this is and is not. It is not an argument against reach, or against the platform, or against the work you did in Module 6. Reach is how people find you, and that job still has to be done. It is simply the reason reach, on its own, cannot be your foundation. A foundation is the part of your marketing that stays standing when the platform changes the rules. Borrowed ground, by definition, is not that part. So the rest of this module builds the part that is.

**8.** If a channel you own is the foundation, the practical question is: which channel? Let me answer it carefully, because this is where the module could go wrong. The usual answer is an email list, and that is a reasonable default. Email is the clearest case of a direct line you genuinely control: you hold the addresses, and no platform decides whether your message arrives. But I want to be precise. The principle is the direct line. The channel is just the means. Email is one means. For some businesses a text-message list is the real one. The point is never the tool, it is the directly-reachable relationship the tool carries. So how do you choose? Two fit questions. First, fit to your audience: which channel will your people actually accept contact on? An audience that ignores email entirely might read a text, and an audience that finds texts intrusive will happily read an email. Choose the channel your people are already comfortable with. || Second, fit to your capacity. This is the one businesses skip. A channel you can genuinely feed once a quarter is worth more than one you start weekly and abandon after a month. Choose the channel sized to the hours you actually have, not the hours you imagine having. Fit, on both counts, beats fashion every time.

**9.** Building a channel you own has two halves. Earning it, and keeping it. This section is the first half: earning it. And it starts from a plain fact. Nobody owes you their attention. A direct line to a person is something they grant you, deliberately. It is not something you take, and it is not something you are owed because your work is good. Before anyone hands you that line, they need a reason. The whole of this section is about what makes that reason real, rather than a trick.

**10.** So what counts as a reason? Let us draw the line clearly, because the hype version and the real version look similar from a distance. The hype version is a bribe. You offer a discount, or a free download, in exchange for an email address. It does work, in the narrow sense that addresses come in. But look at who joins: people who wanted the discount or the download. They wanted the thing. They did not necessarily want you. So the list fills with addresses that came for a one-time item and never open anything again. || The real version is an honest promise of something genuinely worth receiving on an ongoing basis. Useful thinking. Early notice of something that matters to them. A perspective they value and cannot easily get elsewhere. This collects fewer people. That is not a flaw. It collects the right people, the ones who wanted what you actually offer. || And here is the test that separates the two, the one to keep. Would this person stay subscribed after they have received the thing that made them join? If yes, the reason is real, because the reason was you. If no, if the relationship has no purpose once the download is delivered, then you have not built an audience. You have built a funnel, and a funnel empties.

**11.** There is a principle hiding inside that test, and it is worth pulling out, because it connects this section to the next one. The reason to join should be the reason to stay. The same promise. Think about why the bribe fails: a discount gets someone to join, and then what follows, the actual emails, is a completely different promise. There is a gap between the two, and the subscriber falls straight through it. They joined for one thing and got another. When the reason to join and the reason to stay are a single, consistent promise, that gap does not exist. Someone joins because you offer useful thinking, and what arrives is useful thinking. Nothing to fall through. || And this is why earning a channel and keeping a channel are not really two separate problems. The promise that earns you someone’s address is the very same promise you spend the whole relationship keeping. If you get that promise honest and clear at the start, then the next section, sustaining the channel, stops being a question of whether you should and becomes only a question of whether you can. Which has answers. That is where we go next.

**12.** Before we leave earning, the anti-hype principle for this module, stated plainly, because the owned-audience world is louder with bad advice than almost any other. An audience is not a resource you harvest. The loudest advice in this space treats it exactly as a yield to be maximized: capture the most addresses you can, extract the most sales you can, optimize every step of the funnel. The money is in the list, they say. That approach does work, for a while. Here is what it costs. Every aggressive email, every relentless pitch, teaches your audience one lesson: hearing from you means being sold to. And people who learn that lesson stop hearing from you. They unsubscribe, or worse, they simply stop opening, and your channel quietly dies while the address count still looks healthy. An owned audience is not a harvest. It is a group of people who permit you to stay in contact because the contact itself is worth having. That is the whole asset, and it is fragile in one specific way: treat it as a harvest, and you get exactly one season out of it. Treat it as a relationship, and it lasts.

**13.** This section is the second half of the work: keeping the channel. And it opens on the principle that the whole half rests on. A list is collected. An audience is kept. Those are two different things wearing the same set of email addresses. A list is a count. It grows every time you add a name, and it will sit in your software, untouched, for a year, and still be a list. Collecting is the only thing it requires. An audience is not a count. It is a state of relationship. It exists only while the contact between you stays live, and stays worth receiving. The moment that stops being true, you still have a list, but you no longer have an audience. Here is the part to sit with. The same five hundred addresses can be either one. There is no difference in the spreadsheet. The difference is entirely in what you do after people join. Collecting them was a moment, and it is behind you. Keeping them is the work, and it is everything ahead. The rest of this section is that work.

**14.** The moment you genuinely accept that an owned audience is worth building, a very specific fear shows up. It sounds like: that is all well and good, but I would not know what to send them. I do not have a newsletter’s worth of things to say. It is the realest objection in this module, and it has a plain, two-part answer. First, what to send. You do not have to invent anything. You already named the reason people joined, the promise you made them in the last section. What you send is that promise, kept. If they joined for useful thinking on a topic, you send useful thinking on that topic. That is it. You are not obligated to share news, or updates, or everything you know. One useful thing, the thing you promised. The fear assumes a blank page. You do not have a blank page. You have a promise. || Second, how often. Here is the rule: pick the rhythm you could sustain in your busiest realistic month, and commit to exactly that. Not the rhythm you can manage in a calm week. If that honest number is once a quarter, the cadence is once a quarter. A quarterly email you actually send for years beats a weekly one you abandon in five weeks. Under-commit, deliberately. It is the same discipline Module 6 asked of you, and it is right for the same reason: the channel only works if it lasts.

**15.** Now the calibration, and this one needs an honest framing before we start. Everything so far might suggest an owned audience is equally important for every business. It is not. For some businesses it is close to high. For others it is genuinely low, and the right amount of effort to spend on it is small. The next slide walks the five businesses, and you will see the weight vary a lot between them. That variation is not the model failing. The variation is the point. Your job is to find your own honest weight, including the possibility that the honest answer for you is light.

**16.** Five businesses. The same idea, a channel you own. Watch how differently it weighs. Maya, the therapist. For her an owned audience is medium, and it is quiet. Her professional ethics rule out anything that looks like promotion, so her channel cannot be a marketing tool. What it can be is a low-key, occasional professional update to past clients and the peers who refer her. Real, genuinely useful, and deliberately restrained. The constraint is not a problem to solve. It defines the channel. || Marcus, the pressure washer. For him, an owned audience is low, and the honest move is to say so plainly. His work is repeat local jobs, transactional, seasonal. He does not need a standing audience he is in ongoing relationship with. A simple list of past customers, used for the occasional seasonal reminder, is the honest ceiling for him. Building anything more elaborate would be effort spent against the grain of how his business actually works. Do not force it. || Lena, the wedding photographer. For her it is medium, but only once it is redefined. A wedding couple is a client exactly once. So her owned audience is not past couples treated as future buyers, that would be pointless. It is past couples treated as referrers, and it is the network of planners and venues she works alongside. Same tool, completely different audience than the obvious one. || James, the chiropractor. Low. His patients come for a defined course of treatment and then, correctly, they finish and leave. An owned channel for him is not a machine for keeping clients coming back. It is a soft professional presence, a quiet reason he stays in mind if something recurs later. Subtle, and worth a little, not a lot. || Sasha, the vivids stylist. For her, an owned audience is high. This is the strongest case of the five. Her audience discovers her and then follows her work for months before they ever book. That gap, months of attention before a decision, is exactly the situation an owned channel is built for. A direct line to those people is the most natural fit here, and it carries the most weight. Five businesses, and the honest answer ranged from high to low. Find yours.

**17.** Now the same five businesses, but borrowed. We said earlier that borrowed reach is limited but real. Here is what it looks like for keeping the people who already know you, the ones most likely to return or refer. Owned reach is intentional: you choose when to show up. Borrowed reach is ambient: it catches people when they are not looking. Maya, the therapist. Ethics limit her here too, so her borrowed presence is a professional profile and the peers who refer her, not a social feed. || Marcus, the pressure washer. Local Facebook groups and his Google listing, where his name resurfaces when a neighbor asks. || Lena, the wedding photographer. Instagram is her borrowed channel, a past couple’s friend scrolls, sees the work, and remembers her when they get engaged. || James, the chiropractor. Local community pages, where a former patient sees his name and books again when something flares up. || Sasha, the vivids stylist. For her, borrowed is where everything happens, someone follows for months, sees a post, and finally books. So the pattern holds. Own the channel you count on. Keep a presence on the ones you do not. That is where someone scrolling past thinks, I miss them, and comes back.

**18.** One principle to close this section, about size, because the instinct to grow is the instinct most likely to mislead you here. Build the audience you can answer, not the one you can capture. Those are different numbers, and the gap between them is dangerous. It is genuinely easy to collect more people than you can stay in honest contact with. And it feels like progress, because the number goes up. But a large audience you have gone quiet on is not a head start. It is a debt. It is a room full of people you made a promise to and then stopped keeping. Every one of those silent contacts is a small broken promise, and a list of broken promises is worth less than no list at all. So the right size for an owned audience is not set by how many addresses you can gather. It is set by how much real contact you can sustain. If you can keep a genuine promise to two hundred people, then two hundred is your right size, and two thousand is a liability. A smaller audience you actually keep is, straightforwardly, the larger asset. Size to what you can answer.

**19.** If this module is starting to feel like a large new project, here is what takes the weight off. You are almost certainly not starting from zero. Think honestly about it: you have past clients. You have a professional network. You have people who already follow your work on whatever platform Module 6 sent you to. The raw material of an owned audience is already in your hands. It is just scattered, and unorganized, and not yet treated as anything. So an owned audience, for an established business, usually does not begin by gathering strangers. It begins by organizing the people you already have. That is a much smaller act than building a list from nothing, and it is the honest first move. || The smallest real version of this foundation is three things. One channel, chosen for fit. The people already within reach of it, the ones you already have. And a cadence you are genuinely sure you can hold. That is a complete foundation. It is allowed to be small. It can grow later. What it cannot do is grow if it never gets built. So build the small version. That is what the tool is for.

**20.** Time to do the work, right here on this slide. Three decisions, and every one of them is sized to the capacity you genuinely have, not the capacity you wish you had. First, the people. Who do you start with? Not strangers you will gather, the people already within reach: past clients, your network, the people who already follow your work. Name that group in the first row. Second, the channel. One channel you will own. Run it through the two fit questions: where will these people genuinely accept contact, and what can you realistically sustain? Usually that is email. Sometimes it is not. Pick the one, and write it in the second row. Third, the cadence. The rhythm you could hold in your busiest realistic month. Be honest, and be willing to under-commit. Quarterly that you keep beats weekly that you drop. Write that cadence in the third row. Three rows: the people, the channel, the cadence. And remember the calibration, your honest answer might be that this foundation is light for your business, and if so, a small entry here is the correct entry. Everything you set here pulls forward when you reach Module 8. It does not have to be elaborate. It has to be real, and it has to be something you will actually do.

**21.** That is Module 7. An owned audience is no longer a vague influencer idea you had already dismissed. It is a clear thing: a direct line to the right people, one you control, built from people you already have, kept at a cadence you can sustain. Look at how far the foundation has come. You can be found, through awareness. And now you can reach the people who found you, directly, on your own terms. That is a real and durable position. It leaves one question open. When you do reach them, what do they think? Being reachable is not the same as being trusted. You can have a direct line to someone and still not be the person whose judgment they rely on in your field. That last distinction, being known and reachable versus being regarded as the genuine expert, is the subject of the final module. Module 8 is Authority Building, and it also closes the course and assembles everything you have built into one plan. I will see you there.

## Module 8: Authority Building

**1.** This is Module 8, Authority Building. It is the seventh and final foundation, and the last module of the course. This module does two things. First, it teaches authority: being trusted as the genuine expert in your field, which is the last of the seven foundations and the third one in the Be Remembered layer. Then, in its final section, it closes the course. Every module so far has had you mark something in the Marketing Foundation Map. Module 8 pulls all of that together into one thing: your Foundation Plan. That plan is what you leave the course holding. So this module teaches, and then it assembles. By the end you will understand what authority actually is, you will know which kind is yours, and you will have a single, prioritized plan for building the whole foundation, sized to the hours you really have.

**2.** Here is where most people start with the word authority. They flinch from it. And the flinch has a few layers. The top layer: calling yourself an authority feels like bragging, like claiming something it is not your place to claim. The next layer: the people who do call themselves authorities, the thought leaders, often look like they are performing, and you do not want to be that. Both of those are real. But underneath both of them is a third belief, quieter and deeper, and it is the one this module is really here for. It is the sense that you have not earned the title yet. That an authority is a particular kind of person, more senior, more established, further along than you, and you are not that person. You have paying clients and years of work behind you, and still the word authority feels like it belongs to someone else. I want to take that belief apart, because it rests on a specific mistake about what authority actually is. That is the next slide.

**3.** Here is what authority actually is. Authority is earned trust. It is the weight your word carries in your field. Be concrete about that. When you tell a client what you think they should do, one of two things happens. Either they treat it as one opinion among several, to be weighed against others. Or they treat it as the answer, and they move on it. The difference between those two responses is authority. It is not a feeling, and it is not a title. It is the weight your judgment carries with the people who hear it. || And notice what follows. If authority is the weight your word carries with other people, then it was never something you could qualify for, or award yourself, or reach by being senior enough. It is granted. Other people grant it to you, and they grant it based on one thing: whether your judgment, over time, has been sound. Here is the part that takes the earlier belief apart. Your clients have already been doing this. Every client who took your recommendation and acted on it granted your judgment weight. You have been accumulating authority, quietly, for as long as you have had clients. You did not feel it happening because nobody handed you a title. But it is real, and it is already underway. This module is about seeing that clearly, and then building on it deliberately.

**4.** Authority is earned trust. But earned trust is not built the same way by everyone, and this is where a lot of authority advice goes wrong: it assumes one path. There are three kinds of authority. Three different bases on which people come to trust your judgment. They are not three steps, and they are not a ranking. They are three kinds, and most businesses lead with one. Knowing which one is yours is what turns authority from a vague aspiration into a clear instruction. The next two slides take the first two kinds.

**5.** The three kinds of authority. Craft, expertise, and track record. Craft authority is trust earned by the visible quality of the work itself. When the work is good enough, and distinct enough, it makes the case before you say a word. People see what you have made, and the seeing is what produces the trust. || Expertise authority is trust earned by defined, demonstrable knowledge. Here it is not primarily the output people respond to, it is the depth behind it. People rely on your word because of what you reliably, and provably, know about a specific area. || Track-record authority is trust earned by a history of calls that proved right. This one is not about your skill or your knowledge in isolation. It is about your read. People have watched you assess situations and call them correctly, enough times, that they now trust the read itself. || Two things to hold. Most businesses lead with one of the three, not all of them, and that is correct. And these are not ranked. Track record is the slowest to build, which we will come to, but slowest is not best. The right kind is the one that fits your work. We will take craft and expertise now, track record in the next section.

**6.** Craft authority, up close. This is authority that comes from the work itself being visibly, distinctly good. One distinction matters here, because it keeps craft authority separate from something you already learned. In Module 4, earned proof, the trust came from other people vouching for you: testimonials, peer referrals. Craft authority is not that. It is people seeing the work directly and judging it themselves. Not someone telling them you are good. Them looking, and concluding it. The craft is the argument, made without an intermediary. || How is it earned? It starts with doing excellent work, but that is only the start, and this is the part businesses miss. Excellent work that no one sees builds no authority at all. Craft authority is earned by making the quality legible: letting the work be seen, clearly, and often, by the people whose trust would matter. The doing and the showing are both required. || Where it leads is a particular and valuable place. People arrive to talk to you already trusting you, because they have already seen what you do. The judgment has been granted before the first conversation even starts. For a business whose work is visual or demonstrable, this is often the most natural kind of authority to build.

**7.** Expertise authority. This is authority that rests on defined, demonstrable knowledge: a real depth in a specific area. The key words there are defined and demonstrable. Expertise authority is not a general air of knowing things. It is depth in something specific enough to be named, and it is knowledge others can actually verify, not just take on faith. That is what makes it solid. || How is it earned? Not, mostly, by announcing your credentials, though credentials can be part of it. It is earned by making the knowledge visible in the ordinary course of how you work. The question you think to ask that no one else thought of. The distinction you draw that turns out to matter. The thing you noticed that a less expert person would have missed. Every time your depth shows up in the work itself, the expertise becomes visible, and visible expertise is what builds the authority. || Where it leads: people start bringing you the hard cases. The complicated ones, the ones that genuinely need someone who knows. And your word starts to settle questions that other people leave open. For a business built on a real, specific body of knowledge, this is the natural kind.

**8.** The anti-hype principle for this module, and it is the most important one in the course, because authority is the most hype-saturated topic of all. There is a loud version of authority, and it is everywhere. It is a performance. The thought-leader pose. The word expert applied to oneself, by oneself. Confidence turned up and up until it reads, at a glance, as credibility. A whole industry teaches this performance, and it is worth being clear about what it can and cannot do. It can draw a crowd. Performance is genuinely good at attracting attention. What it cannot do is draw trust. And the reason is the definition we started with. Authority is earned trust, and trust is a verdict. It is something other people reach, on their own, by watching whether your judgment actually holds up over time. A verdict is not yours to issue. You cannot award yourself the thing that, by its nature, only other people can grant. So the announced version and the real version are not two flavors of the same thing. One is a costume and one is the body underneath it. The performance can look like authority from a distance and it builds none. The rest of this module is about the real thing, the granted thing, and the good news is that the real thing is built by work you are already doing.

**9.** Two kinds of authority so far: craft and expertise. One is left, track record, and it is the deepest of the three. And then this section turns to the question that actually matters for you: of the three kinds, which is yours? Not which would be impressive to have. Which one are you, in fact, already earning, so that building authority becomes a matter of continuing something rather than starting something. We will look at track record first, then at how to find your own kind.

**10.** Track-record authority. The third kind, and the deepest. This is authority that comes from a track record of calls that proved right. It is worth being precise about how it differs from the other two. Craft is about the quality of what you make. Expertise is about the depth of what you know. Track record is about your read: your ability to look at a situation, often an uncertain one, and call which way it will go. And to have done that, visibly, enough times to be right. || How is it earned? Slowly. There is no shortcut, and that is not a flaw in your approach, it is the nature of the thing. Track-record authority can only be built by being right, in view of other people, over time. It needs history. That is also what makes it the hardest kind to fake: a performance cannot manufacture a track record. The years are the proof. || Where it leads is the deepest form of trust there is. People consult you before they decide, not after. They do not bring you a defined job to execute. They bring you the situation itself and want your read on it first. If you have been in your field a long time and clients increasingly ask what you think before they ask what you charge, that is track-record authority, and it has been forming whether or not you named it.

**11.** So: which kind is yours? And the framing of that question matters. You are not standing at a menu choosing a kind of authority to begin building from zero. You already have one. You have been earning it. The task is to identify which. The clearest signal is not in how you see yourself. It is in what your best clients say when they explain, to you or to someone else, why they chose you. Listen to the actual words. If what they point to is the work, look at what she made, look at the results, then your authority is craft. They are responding to the visible quality of the output, and the way you build from there is to make sure the work keeps being seen. If what they point to is your knowing, she really understands this, she knew exactly what the issue was, then your authority is expertise. They are responding to your depth, and you build from there by letting that depth stay visible in how you work. If what they point to is your read, she told me this would happen and it did, she was right, then your authority is track record. They are responding to that history, and you build from there by continuing to make calls where others can see them land. Most of you will recognize one of those three clearly. That recognition is the instruction. It tells you not just what kind of authority you have, but the specific way you build more of it.

**12.** A principle that ties the three kinds together, and it follows straight from granted, not announced. Whichever kind of authority is yours, it is shown the same way: by doing, not by describing. Craft authority is shown by letting the work be seen, not by calling the work excellent. Expertise authority is shown in the question you think to ask that no one else did, not by listing what you know. Track-record authority is shown by making calls where other people can watch them land, not by claiming a good history. In every case, the demonstration is the work itself, made visible. None of the three kinds is ever shown by describing yourself. This is the practical core of the whole module. You do not build authority by adding a layer of self-description on top of your work. You build it by doing the work well, in a way that can be seen. The work is the argument. Your only job, beyond doing it, is to make sure it is visible.

**13.** The calibration. Five businesses, and we look at which kind of authority each one leads with. Two things to watch for. First, the kind genuinely varies, you will see craft, expertise, and track record all show up. Second, and this matters, the amount varies too. For one of the five, authority is low, and the honest thing is to say so plainly rather than pretend every business needs to become a recognized expert. The variation, including the small answer, is the point.

**14.** Five businesses. Which kind of authority, and how much it weighs. Maya, the therapist. Her authority is expertise. The trust clients and referring professionals place in her rests on clinical depth, on genuinely knowing her area, and on the regard of her peers. And note how it is shown: not through self-promotion, which her ethics rule out anyway, but in the precision of how she works, the questions she asks, the care of her judgment. For Maya authority is high. It is central to why people choose her. || Marcus, the pressure washer. For Marcus, authority is low, and the honest move is to say so. A pressure washer is not building thought leadership, and pretending otherwise would be a waste of his limited hours. His honest ceiling is a thin slice of craft authority: being reliably trusted, locally, to do a good job. That is real, it is worth having, and it is small. Naming it as low is the correct answer for him, not a failure of the model. || Lena, the wedding photographer. Her authority is craft. Her documentary style is distinct, and it is visible, every wedding she shoots is the argument for the next one. The work speaks. She builds authority simply by making sure that distinct work keeps being seen by the right people. || James, the chiropractor. Like Maya, expertise, but in his own defined area. Patients trust him, and the GPs who refer to him trust him, because of what he demonstrably knows about a specific kind of case. His depth, made visible in how he treats and explains, is the authority. || Sasha, the vivids stylist. Craft authority, with a particular trajectory. Her vivid colour work is unmistakable, instantly recognizable as hers. And craft authority, when the work is that distinct and seen that often, turns into something more: a named specialism.
